Why Judges Won't Use AI: The Real Reason
A tired judge is a bad judge. This should be obvious, but the legal profession has spent decades dressing up exhaustion as conscientiousness. Judges are not overworked because they are doing their jobs. They are overworked because they are making bad sausage and calling it justice. The standard objection to AI in adjudication is that the technology will get things wrong. This is a lie. The real fear is that AI will get things right, and a judiciary that gets things right cannot log-roll with the lawyers who appear in its courtroom every week.
The Mafia-Don Model
The mafia-don model of judging depends entirely on the gap between the law as written and the law as administered. AI closes that gap. Consider what a competent retrieval-grounded system does when placed next to the bench. It flags every dispositive argument the movant raised. It checks whether the order actually addresses those arguments. It catches mischaracterizations of the record in seconds. None of the standard evasions survive.
Consider seven scenarios that play out in courtrooms every day, and what AI would do to each one.
Scenario one. A pro se litigant files a thirty-page motion documenting that the trustee liquidated a portfolio one day before a tax-year cutoff, generating four hundred thousand dollars in avoidable capital gains. The opposing firm files a two-page response that does not address the timing at all. The judge denies the motion in a one-paragraph order citing "the trustee's broad discretion." An AI auditor flags within seconds that the timing argument was never engaged. The denial cannot survive the flag. Today, no flag exists, and the denial stands.
Scenario two. A lawyer tells the court at oral argument that a third-party bank "did not include a date on the correspondence" in question. The correspondence in fact bears a clear date, which the lawyer has scrubbed before submission. The judge accepts the representation and rules accordingly. An AI cross-check against the original exhibit catches the discrepancy instantly. Today, the lie is caught only if the opposing party happens to notice and has the resources to file a separate motion about it.
Scenario three. A trustee submits a sworn accounting listing one bank account. A subpoena response from the bank lists six accounts in the trust's name. The judge, presented with both documents, rules that the discrepancy is "a matter for further discovery" and proceeds to grant the trustee's motion to dismiss. An AI auditor flags that a sworn accounting omitting eighty-three percent of the trust's accounts is not a discovery issue but a perjury issue and a fiduciary breach on its face. Today, the "further discovery" formulation is a standard tool for kicking dispositive evidence down the road until the litigant runs out of money.
Scenario four. A firm represents both the trustee and a beneficiary whose interests are adverse to the trust. The pro se litigant files a motion to disqualify under MRPC 1.7. The firm responds with a boilerplate denial. The judge rules that the conflict is "speculative" without analyzing the actual fee arrangements or the specific adverse interests. An AI system applies the rule to the facts and produces a four-page analysis showing the conflict is structural and non-waivable. Today, "speculative" is a magic word that ends the inquiry.
Scenario five. A litigant files an emergency motion documenting that opposing counsel executed a settlement fourteen days after the client explicitly rejected it in writing. The court schedules a hearing six months out. By the time the hearing occurs, the unauthorized settlement has been performed and the funds dispersed. An AI docket-management system flags that an emergency motion involving completed unauthorized acts requires immediate scheduling under the court's own rules. Today, slow-walking is itself the remedy the offending party receives.
Scenario six. A judge issues a ruling that calls the grounds for a third-party bank freeze "immaterial." The freeze was instituted because the trustee was attempting to wire herself trust funds. An AI legal-reasoning audit produces a memorandum showing that materiality in trust litigation is tethered to fiduciary breach, that a freeze blocking self-dealing is direct evidence of self-dealing, and that no competent first-year associate would write the word "immaterial" in this context. Today, "immaterial" is a verdict that requires no analysis to support it.
Scenario seven. A large firm appears before the same judge thirty times a year. A pro se litigant appears once. The firm's filings are accepted at face value; the litigant's filings are scrutinized for procedural defects and dismissed on technicalities. An AI review system applied uniformly to both sides produces identical scrutiny standards regardless of who filed. Today, the asymmetry is the entire mechanism by which repeat players win cases they should lose on the merits.
How the Lawyers Benefit
This is where the canard about AI vitiating "legitimate craft" becomes most transparent. The lawyers benefit from the current system in ways they will never articulate in print, but the structure is straightforward.
A practicing litigator's value to a client is largely a function of the litigator's relationships with the bench and with opposing counsel. The substantive legal work, the part that AI does well, is a small fraction of the billable hour. The larger fraction is navigation, which means knowing which judge will accept which arguments, which clerk to call, which firm not to antagonize, and which procedural lever to pull to delay a case until the other side breaks. None of this is craft in any meaningful sense. It is rent extraction from a system whose opacity is the source of the rent.
If AI flattens the playing field, the rent disappears. A pro se litigant with a strong record becomes as effective as a firm charging eight hundred dollars an hour, because the merits become legible without the navigation. The firm's value proposition collapses. This is the actual threat, and it is why the loudest resistance to legal AI comes from bar associations rather than from litigants.
The "AI hallucinates" objection is real but vastly overstated, and in any case it applies with equal force to exhausted human judges who hallucinate materiality determinations on a daily basis. The "due process" objection is real but addressable through retrieval-grounded systems and disclosure protocols. Neither objection explains the intensity of the resistance. The intensity is explained only by the threat to the rent.
The Reputational Economy
The federal bench operates on a reputational economy that has almost no quality-control mechanism. Appeals are rare, reversals rarer, and most rulings are never read by anyone with the standing to grade them. A judge can issue thirty years of unreasoned orders and retire as a respected jurist, because the product is never audited. Judge Chuang was called distinguished, but his rulings read like the work of a C-student who barely passed the exam. Judge Lease earned a clean F. AI would audit the product. That is precisely why the bench resists it.
The honest version of the objection has never been spoken aloud, but it goes like this. We have built careers around the discretion to not enforce the law as written. AI would enforce it. Therefore AI is dangerous. The litigants would benefit. The guild would not. That tells you everything.
