The Actual Nature of Implicit Knowledge
Proof that Epistemic Engineering Must Replace Epistemology
There is a debate, centuries old, concerning the nature of so-called "implicit" knowledge. A baseball player catches a fly ball. To do so, he must, in some sense, "know" the laws of physics—or at least know enough about projectile motion, air resistance, and his own kinematics to position himself correctly. But he obviously does not know these laws in the way that Newton knew them. He cannot state them. He cannot apply them deductively. He cannot, in any straightforward sense, be said to be consulting them. And yet his behavior is law-compliant to a degree that mere chance cannot explain. What, then, is going on?
Two answers have dominated the literature, and both are wrong.
The first answer, which I will call the intellectualist answer, holds that the laws are explicitly encoded somewhere in the player's nervous system—not in his ego, not in his conscious mind, but in some subpersonal substrate that mediates his behavior. On this view, there is a "little Newton" inside the player, performing the relevant calculations and issuing motor commands accordingly. The player himself is unaware of this Newton, just as a dog is unaware of the laws governing synaptic transmission in its own brain. But the Newton is there, and his deductions are what generate the player's behavior.
The second answer, associated with Wittgenstein and Searle, holds that this picture is absurd. There is no little Newton. There is no encoded representation of any law. There is only physiology—reflexes, dispositions, trained responses. To speak of "implicit knowledge" is to commit the homunculus fallacy. The player does not know the laws of physics in any sense whatsoever. He just reacts, and his reactions happen to be law-compliant because evolution and training have shaped his reflexes that way. End of story.
Both answers are false, and they are false in instructive ways.
The intellectualist answer is false for the reasons Wittgenstein gave. A rule, explicitly represented, has no regulatory power over the system in which it is represented. It is just another token. To apply the rule, the system needs another rule telling it how to apply the first rule, and so on into regress. An encoded Newton's law sitting in the baseball player's nervous system is just a string of symbols; it does no more work than a copy of Principia Mathematica sitting on his nightstand. The regulatory force that the intellectualist needs cannot be supplied by inscription.
The Wittgenstein-Searle answer is false for a different reason. It cannot account for the productive, flexible, unbounded character of skilled performance. The baseball player does not catch fly balls by running through a finite repertoire of trained reflexes; he catches fly balls he has never seen before, in conditions he has never encountered, and he does so reliably. Pure reflex theories collapse under the weight of this productivity. And the Wittgenstein-Searle view also fails to distinguish between the cognitive and the intellectual. Skilled performance is cognitive—it is intelligent, context-sensitive, generative—without being intellectual in the sense of involving conscious rule-application. The reflex theorist conflates these and concludes that, because skilled performance is not intellectual, it must not be cognitive either. This is a non-sequitur.
The correct answer—the third position that the debate requires—is this. Skilled performance consists of intelligent local reactions to specific situations. These reactions are not reflexes; they are sensitive to features, responsive to relevance, generative in their application. But they are also not derived from any general law. The baseball player is not deducing his behavior from Newton's laws; he is reacting locally to the trajectory of a particular ball. His reactions are law-compliant not because the laws are generating them but because the laws describe the very world to which the reactions are responding. Compliance is downstream of local intelligent reaction, not its source.
And as the web of local reactions grows—as the player encounters more balls, more trajectories, more conditions—the reactions become mutually constraining. A reaction at one point is no longer purely local; it has to fit with neighboring reactions. The system develops what can only be called semi-non-local knowledge: knowledge that is implicit in the shape of the constraint surface that the local reactions collectively define, but that is not tokened anywhere within the system. The system "knows" the laws in the sense that its reactions form a law-shaped manifold. But no point on the manifold contains the laws.
This third position is not a speculation. It is borne out, with unusual clarity, by the actual architecture and behavior of contemporary AI systems.
Consider large language models. Nowhere in a transformer's weights is there a tokened representation of Bayes' theorem, or modus ponens, or the principle of charity. Yet the outputs of these models are, to a striking degree, Bayes-compliant, logic-compliant, and charity-compliant. What training does is not inscribe rules; it shapes a high-dimensional embedding space such that, for any local input, the next-token distribution is a context-sensitive reaction that has been pulled, over billions of gradient updates, into alignment with the patterns that the relevant laws describe. The model is not consulting Bayes. It is reacting locally, and its reaction has been sculpted by exposure to a corpus in which Bayes-compliant patterns are pervasive. Compliance is the consequence of shaped local reactivity, not the cause of it.
The mutual-constraint dimension shows up concretely in the architecture itself. Attention heads make each token's representation a function of its neighbors, and across layers these representations become increasingly contextualized. A local reaction at position N is shaped by, and shapes, reactions at neighboring positions, which are in turn shaped by their neighbors. By the upper layers, no single activation is purely local; each carries the imprint of a wide constraint structure. This is the third position made silicon: semi-non-local knowledge coded into the system without being explicitly tokened anywhere.
The dispositive evidence comes from what happens when engineers try the intellectualist approach. Attempts to inject explicit rules—system prompts saying "follow Bayes," hardcoded logical axioms, rule-based guardrails—routinely fail. The injected rules are treated as just more text. They have nothing like the regulatory force of the shaped embedding geometry, and the model ignores them or rationalizes around them. The rule, tokened, is noise. The rule, geometrized, is law. This is exactly what Wittgenstein predicted would happen to explicit rules, and exactly what the third position predicts will not happen to shaped dispositional geometry. The Wittgensteinian critique of the intellectualist picture is correct; the Wittgensteinian alternative is not.
The history of computer chess shows the same pattern even more cleanly. Deep Blue, which defeated Kasparov in 1997, was Fork 1 incarnate. Engineers consulted grandmasters, extracted heuristics—control the center, knights before bishops, rooks on open files—and encoded them as weighted terms in an explicit evaluation function. The system was a calculator with grandmaster knowledge inscribed into it. It played well, but no one thought it understood chess. It was the intellectualist picture in silicon.
AlphaZero, twenty years later, was the third position in silicon. DeepMind gave it the rules of chess and nothing else. No opening book, no grandmaster games, no positional heuristics, no hand-crafted evaluation. It played itself millions of times, with a neural network learning to evaluate positions. After nine hours of self-play it was crushing Stockfish, the inheritor of the Deep Blue tradition. And critically, AlphaZero's chess knowledge is nowhere tokened. It does not have a "control the center" rule; it has a network whose evaluations of center-controlling positions are systematically higher. Grandmasters described its play as alien—it sacrificed material for long-term positional pressure in ways no human framework predicted. Which is precisely what one would expect if the system were generating intelligent local reactions to specific positions rather than deducing moves from general principles. Leela Chess Zero, the open-source successor, independently rediscovered most of the classical principles of chess strategy through bottom-up training alone. The principles are the manifold. They are not the rules generating the manifold.
The verdict is decisive and the verdict is empirical. Bottom-up systems dominate top-down systems in every domain in which they have been seriously tested. This is not a contingent fact about engineering; it is a structural fact about cognition. Implicit knowledge is not encoded knowledge that happens to be inaccessible to the ego. It is the shape of a dispositional manifold sculpted by local intelligent reactions under mutual constraint. There is no Newton in the baseball player's nervous system, and there is no Bayes in the language model's weights. There is a geometry, and the geometry does the work that inscription was supposed to do but could not.
This has a consequence that the philosophical literature has not yet absorbed. Epistemology, conceived as the analysis of knowledge in terms of justified belief plus appropriate connection to truth, presupposes the intellectualist picture. It presupposes that knowledge is the kind of thing that can be stated, defended, and connected by inferential threads to its objects. But most of the knowledge that actually does cognitive work—in baseball players, in chess players, in language users, in artificial systems—is not of this kind. It is geometric, not propositional. It is sculpted, not inscribed. And the methods appropriate to its study are not the methods of conceptual analysis; they are the methods of engineering. One does not analyze a manifold by interrogating its inhabitants. One reverse-engineers it by studying how it was shaped and what it now does.
Epistemic engineering must therefore replace epistemology, not as an act of intellectual rebellion but as a matter of subject-matter fit. The objects of epistemology, as classically conceived, are a vanishingly small subset of the objects of cognition. The remainder—the vast majority—are objects for engineering. The question is no longer what justifies a belief but what shape of dispositional geometry yields law-compliant local reactions. The question is no longer whether knowledge is justified true belief but how a sculpted manifold can do the regulatory work that propositions were supposed to do and never actually did. This is not a deflation of epistemology. It is a recognition that epistemology was studying the foam on the wave and mistaking it for the ocean.
