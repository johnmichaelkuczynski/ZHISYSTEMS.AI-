Why AI Will Not Replace Judges (Even Though It Should): Calum Chace's Blind Spot
Calum Chace is right about almost everything in The Economic Singularity. He is right that AI is overtaking human cognitive labor across every measurable dimension. He is right that the displacement will be wider and faster than the optimists admit. He is right that capitalism in its current form depends on labor markets that AI is in the process of dismantling. He is even right about the law in the narrow sense that AI adjudication is already better than human adjudication on every metric that can be honestly measured. Where he is wrong, and wrong in a way that matters, is in assuming that the legal profession will be displaced by the same forces that are displacing travel agents, paralegals, and entry-level coders. It will not. The legal profession is structurally immune to those forces, and understanding why is the key to understanding why braindead judges and scum lawyers will still be running the courthouse a half-century from now while every other knowledge sector has been hollowed out.
The Law Is Not a Profession; It Is a Monopoly
Chace's implicit model of professional displacement runs through market discipline. A travel agent loses her job because Expedia gives the customer direct access to the underlying inventory. A junior coder loses his job because the firm employing him faces a competitor that uses AI and undercuts him on price. The mechanism in every case is the same. A buyer with a choice picks the cheaper, faster, better option, and the incumbent who cannot match it dies. This is how technological displacement has worked for two hundred years.
The legal system is built specifically to neutralize this mechanism. There is no buyer with a choice, because access to the courts is not a market. Litigants cannot shop for adjudication the way travelers shop for flights. The judge assigned to the case is the judge, and there is no competing judge offering the same service for less. The bar admission requirement means non-lawyers cannot offer legal services at any price, no matter how competent. The unauthorized practice statutes are criminal in most jurisdictions. The appellate process is controlled by the same guild that produced the trial-level error, and the deference standards are calibrated to ensure that most errors stick. There is no competitive pressure point at which a better technology can enter. The system is sealed.
Half the Law Is About How the Law Cannot Be Displaced
This is the part that technologists writing about legal automation almost never grasp, because it is invisible from outside. A staggering fraction of legal doctrine is not about resolving disputes between parties. It is about maintaining the monopoly of the system that resolves disputes between parties. Standing doctrine determines who is allowed to bring a case. Sovereign immunity determines who cannot be sued. Judicial immunity determines that a judge cannot be sued for ruling wrongly, no matter how egregious the ruling. Abstention doctrines determine which courts can hear which cases. Exhaustion requirements force litigants through layers of administrative process before they can reach a court. The political question doctrine excludes whole categories of dispute from judicial review. The contempt power lets a judge jail anyone who challenges his authority in real time. The rule-making authority of the bar determines who is allowed to appear in court at all.
None of this exists to produce better adjudication. All of it exists to ensure that the system, whatever its quality, cannot be routed around. A litigant frustrated by an incompetent judge cannot fire him and hire a competent one. A litigant frustrated by an abusive lawyer cannot replace the lawyer with an AI system that drafts better motions, because the AI system cannot file the motions itself. The litigant must go through the guild, and the guild controls the terms of access. This is not a flaw in the system; it is the system. The maintenance of the monopoly is the system's primary function. Adjudication is a side effect.
The Median Judge Is Not Tired; He Is Bored
Chace's model assumes that judges, like doctors and engineers, face cognitive demands that AI could meaningfully relieve. This is true of perhaps a few hundred appellate specialists working on genuinely hard interpretive questions. The Second Circuit panel reading UCC Article 9 against a novel fact pattern would benefit enormously from AI assistance, and would probably welcome it, because the work is hard and the ego investment is in getting it right.
The other ninety-nine percent of the bench is not in this position. The trial judge running a trust docket or a landlord-tenant docket or a criminal motions docket is not wrestling with hard questions. He has seen the same fact pattern ten thousand times. He applies the same shortcuts. He rules in whatever direction clears the calendar fastest, gives the appearance of being a considered ruling, and is unlikely to be reversed on appeal. The cognitive demand is near zero. What is high is the ceremonial demand, the demand of being addressed as Your Honor, of having counsel rise when he enters, of issuing pronouncements that no one is permitted to challenge in real time. The robe is not a costume worn during demanding intellectual work. The robe is the work.
This is why AI is not experienced by the median judge as a labor-saving tool but as an existential threat. AI does not relieve him of any burden he actually feels. He does not feel overwhelmed; he feels important. What AI threatens is precisely the thing he values, which is the unauditable nature of his pronouncements. A system that produces a four-page analysis demonstrating that his one-paragraph denial failed to engage the movant's arguments is not helpful to him. It is an indictment. The labor it would save is the labor of pretending the one-paragraph denial was the product of judgment rather than fatigue or favoritism or contempt for the litigant. He does not want this labor saved. He wants it preserved, because preserving it is what preserves him.
The Lawyers Will Capture the Technology
The smarter members of the bar understand exactly what AI can do and have already begun the work of capturing it. The pattern is the same as it has been with every previous technology that threatened the guild. The guild does not resist the technology outright. The guild absorbs the technology in roles that increase lawyer productivity while preserving lawyer necessity, and prohibits or restricts it in roles that would allow it to bypass the lawyer.
Document review is now largely automated, and the productivity gain has been pocketed by the firms rather than passed to clients in the form of lower bills. The lawyer remains the gatekeeper. Discovery summarization is automated, with the lawyer still billing for the oversight. Brief drafting is increasingly automated, with the lawyer still signing the brief and charging for the signature. Legal research is automated, with the lawyer still required to file the result. In every case, the technology has made the lawyer faster without making the lawyer optional. The bills have not gone down. The bar has gotten richer.
The roles in which AI would actually threaten the guild, the roles in which it would allow ordinary people to file motions, draft pleadings, oppose lawyers in court, or obtain unmediated adjudication on the merits, are precisely the roles in which AI is being prohibited. State bars across the country are moving to ban AI-assisted pro se filings, on the stated grounds that they constitute the unauthorized practice of law. The unstated grounds are that they would let pro se litigants compete with represented parties on something approaching equal terms, which would collapse the value proposition of representation in routine matters. The guild is moving fast on this front, and it is winning.
The Real Path to Displacement Is Slow and External
None of this means the legal monopoly is permanent. Monopolies do break, including guild monopolies. They just break differently from market-discipline displacement. They break through external pressure, jurisdictional arbitrage, and slow erosion at the edges over decades.
Some smaller country desperate for foreign investment will permit AI-driven commercial arbitration with binding force, and the results will be faster, cheaper, and more predictable than the American system. Capital will route around the American bench the way it already routes around American class-action exposure through Delaware incorporation and pre-dispute arbitration clauses. Sophisticated parties will increasingly contract out of the U.S. court system entirely, because they can. The American bench will be left adjudicating the disputes of people who cannot afford to contract elsewhere, which is to say the people the bench has always treated worst. The monopoly will not break. It will hollow out.
Eventually, perhaps in fifty years, the political constituency for the current legal monopoly will have eroded enough that legislative reform becomes possible. The unauthorized practice statutes will be loosened. AI-assisted pro se practice will be permitted in low-stakes matters first, then in higher-stakes matters as the results turn out to be better than the lawyered alternative. The bar will fight every step. Some battles it will win. The overall trend will be toward the slow displacement of the guild, but on a timeline measured in generations, not decades.
The Bottom Line
Chace is right that AI is better than the human practitioners it would replace. He is right that this is true in law specifically. Where he is wrong is in assuming that being better is sufficient for displacement. In a market, it is. In a guild backed by state power, it is not. The judges who deserve to be rotated out, the ones issuing one-paragraph denials of thirty-page motions, calling forged documents immaterial, treating perjury as a discovery issue, will not be rotated out. They will be retiring on full pensions in the same chambers in 2050. The lawyers who deserve to be priced out by automation will be charging the same rates with AI doing the actual work in the back office. The mystique of the bench will be preserved. The clients will keep paying. The litigants will keep losing on procedural grounds while AI systems sit in vendor offices ready to do the work better, blocked from doing it by rules the guild itself wrote.
This is the part of the singularity that will not arrive on schedule. Chace is reading the capability curve correctly and the institutional curve incorrectly. The institutions designed to be technology-proof will turn out to be technology-proof. The robe survives the algorithm, because the robe was never about the algorithm in the first place.
