Essay 1: The Empty Shelf
The literature of lawyers and judges writing meaningfully about the law does not exist. The shelf is empty. Every apparent occupant of that shelf, examined closely, turns out to be something else — a confession dressed as a critique, a grievance dressed as an analysis, a careerist's in-house product dressed as defection.

Sol Wachtler is the cleanest apparent case. Chief Judge of the New York Court of Appeals, prosecuted in the early 1990s for stalking and extorting a former lover, imprisoned, wrote After the Madness (1997) about the experience. The book is sometimes cited as a real instance of a senior judicial figure achieving genuine insight into the system. It is not. Wachtler was a man who stalked a woman, threatened her and her teenage daughter, and got prosecuted for exactly the conduct the criminal law exists to prosecute. The system worked. The sentence was modest. The book that emerged is a memoir of a man processing his own injustice — the prosecution he experienced as overzealous, the pretrial detention he experienced as cruel, the mental-health handling he experienced as inadequate. Each of those complaints may have local merit; none of them constitutes a recognition that what he had spent forty years doing on the bench was itself a structural fraud. The book is the work of a man who continues to believe he was a good judge who became a victim. The class of people he ground up over four decades on the New York bench never enters the analysis because, in his self-understanding, they were never the subject of his work; they were administrative throughput. The book confirms by its silence what the question was asking about.

Edward Nottingham, federal judge in the District of Colorado, resigned in 2008 after a string of scandals involving prostitutes and a campaign of harassment against a woman who reported him. He wrote nothing of consequence. He left and disappeared. He is sometimes mentioned in lists of "fallen judges" but the falling produced no insight, no document, no testimony. He is on no shelf.

Alex Kozinski, Ninth Circuit, forced into retirement in 2017 after harassment findings involving clerks and other women. He has continued to publish opinion pieces and give occasional interviews, none of which contain anything resembling a recognition of what his conduct meant or what the apparatus that enabled it meant. He has framed himself, when he has spoken, as a man brought down by changing standards. The framing is exactly the role-preserving move that the question was asking whether anyone in this class can escape. He has not escaped it. There is no Kozinski book on the shelf either.

Mark Ciavarella, the Pennsylvania "kids for cash" juvenile judge, was convicted of racketeering for taking payments from a private detention facility in exchange for sending juveniles to it. He has produced nothing. His conduct generated some writing by other judges, none of it sustained or analytically serious. He is a case study in how a judge can commit straightforward bribery and never reach any recognition about the larger machinery he was operating inside. The bribes were the entirety of the scandal. The procedural apparatus that allowed him to ruin thousands of children's lives without bribes is not what he was prosecuted for and not what he has, at any point, reflected on.

Jerome Frank, Second Circuit, wrote Law and the Modern Mind (1930) and Courts on Trial (1949) while drawing a federal judicial salary. The books are the canonical "judge critiquing law" texts in the American legal-realist canon. They cannot be what the question is asking about because Frank was paid by the institution throughout. His critiques are calibrated to the level of generality the institution could tolerate. He describes the indeterminacy of legal rules, the role of judicial personality, the fictions of formalism — all observations the profession can absorb and indeed has absorbed, citing him as evidence of its own self-awareness. He does not name specific corrupt judges. He does not describe specific firms gouging specific estates. He does not describe the operational mechanisms by which his colleagues produced specific harms to specific people. The work is in-house product. It survives in the academic canon precisely because it never threatened the funding source.

The American legal realist movement more broadly fails the same test. Holmes on the Supreme Court. Llewellyn at Columbia. Pound as Dean of Harvard Law. The "critique" was produced entirely by people whose paychecks depended on the system continuing to find the critique acceptable. The movement's enduring legacy in the academy is exactly the inoculation function: law students read realism in their first year, have the "law is politics" moment, process it as an intellectual phase, and proceed to practice law as though it weren't true. Realism is what legal education uses to vaccinate against the recognition the question is asking about.

Duncan Kennedy and the Critical Legal Studies movement fail the same test more severely. Kennedy drew a Harvard Law School salary for his entire career. CLS, as a movement, was a cohort of well-credentialed academics critiquing the profession from inside chaired professorships at the top law schools. The prose is famously bad — abstraction stacked on abstraction, autobiographical asides, jargon-laden generality — because there was never a specific object the writing was trying to describe. The "critique" was a career. The careers were funded by the institutions being critiqued. The accommodation was total. No CLS scholar ever defunded himself to produce work the funding source would not have tolerated.

The legal-aid memoir genre — "Bad Lawyer" type books, lawyers who left the profession or are about to and want to explain why — fails by a different mechanism. These books are produced by people whose grievances are personal-occupational rather than structural. The lawyer didn't make partner. The lawyer found billable hours dehumanizing. The lawyer's spouse wanted them to be home more. The lawyer experienced a particular boss as a bully. None of these are critiques of the law. They are critiques of the lawyer's own employment circumstances. The frame of the law itself is preserved throughout — fair-process, adversarial system, justice generally functioning — with the author's particular experience presented as a local exception. The reader finishes the book and the law is exactly as advertised.

The judicial memoir genre, separately, is almost entirely role-maintenance. Retired judges write books about their distinguished careers, their notable cases, the colleagues they admired, the institution they served. The books are extended advertisements for the role the author occupied. They are written, edited, and published by people whose continuing social standing depends on the role retaining its prestige. They contain nothing analytical because analysis would jeopardize the prestige.

Lynn Stewart, defense lawyer convicted of providing material support to terrorism, sometimes appears on lists of lawyers who became critics of the system. She did become a public commentator on the federal prosecution apparatus after her conviction, and her writing has more bite than most. But the position she occupies in the analysis is that of victim of overzealous prosecution rather than analyst of structural failure, and the specific framing of her case — radical lawyer representing a controversial client, prosecuted under post-9/11 expansion of material support law — limits the generalizability of her insights to her own situation.

The academic literature on the legal profession produced by sociologists, anthropologists, and political scientists who are not themselves lawyers contains real observation, but is not what the question is asking about. Andrew Abbott's The System of Professions (1988) is rigorous on professional jurisdiction and inter-professional competition. The law-and-society journals contain empirical work on courthouse cultures, plea bargaining, judicial behavior, and litigant experience that is sometimes excellent. None of this is lawyers writing about law. It is outsiders studying lawyers as one studies any other social phenomenon. The work exists because the people producing it were not on the legal payroll.

Litigant memoirs and pro-se accounts of court experiences exist, scattered across self-published and small-press venues. Some are documentary and specific. Almost none are written by lawyers. They are written by people the legal system ground up, who decided to write it down. The legal profession does not cite this material, does not assign it, does not engage with it. It is treated as the equivalent of patient memoirs of medical mistreatment — possibly factually accurate at the individual level, but not the kind of thing the profession considers part of the literature on itself.

Investigative journalism on specific judicial corruption produces occasional bursts of useful material. Lincoln Steffens-era muckraking. Local newspaper coverage of specific scandals. Books like Stuart Taylor and KC Johnson's Until Proven Innocent on the Duke lacrosse case. These are works of journalism, not lawyer-authored critique. The journalists were paid by their publications, not by the legal system, and produced specific observations within particular cases. They do not amount to a body of literature by lawyers about law.

The closest thing to genuine ex-lawyer critique tends to come from people who left the profession early, did something else for decades, and wrote from outside the funding apparatus. There are scattered examples — the occasional novelist who passed through law school, the rare memoirist who walked out of the profession in their thirties and never came back, the lapsed prosecutor turned investigator. These are not numerous, they are not organized into a tradition, and they are not on any standard reading list. They are individual departures rather than a literature.

So the inventory, conducted honestly, yields zero. Every named candidate either was being paid by the system when they wrote, was a convicted figure processing personal grievance, was a legal-aid careerist complaining about working conditions, or was a non-lawyer scholar studying lawyers from outside. The category "lawyer or judge writing meaningfully about the law" is empty. The shelf has the appearance of being stocked because the profession has produced an enormous quantity of writing about itself, but every item on the shelf, examined, turns out to be something else.

This is the fact. The analysis of why this is the fact comes in the essays that follow. The fact itself, logged without explanation, is that the literature does not exist.

Essay 2: The Catechism of the Pseudo-Defector
The lawyers who present themselves as critics of the law repeat a small number of claims that are not critiques but advertisements. These claims have become so standard that they function as a catechism: the entry-level performance of mild dissent that establishes the speaker's bona fides as a thoughtful insider while leaving every structural feature of the profession untouched. Three are worth examining in detail.

The first is the claim that lawyers are "competitive." The kid of the lawyer says it at the dinner table — "my dad is super competitive, that's why he became a lawyer." The lawyer-YouTuber says it as a self-description. The legal-aid memoirist says it about the colleagues she is supposedly criticizing — "the firm I left was hyper-competitive, that wasn't the kind of lawyer I wanted to be." The word does specific rhetorical work and the work is fraudulent.

The insinuation behind "competitive" is that the lawyer fights for his client. That when retained, he becomes an instrument of the client's interest, willing to outwork and outthink the opposing side to deliver a result the client wants. The image is of the gun-for-hire, the hired blade, the professional who will go further for his client than the client could go for himself. This image is what the profession sells, and what the kid of the lawyer is repeating when he describes his dad as competitive.

The image is false at every level. Lawyers do not score clean victories. They do not, in the overwhelming majority of cases, do anything that an external observer could identify as winning. What they do is delay, obfuscate, file motions, request continuances, negotiate settlements neither party can independently evaluate, and produce paper that closes out files in ways that allow both sides' lawyers to bill and both sides' clients to be told that something was achieved. The "competition" is between lawyers who share a common interest in keeping the apparatus running. The opposing lawyer is not the enemy. The opposing lawyer is the colleague at the next conference table, the person whose continued retention depends on the file remaining alive long enough to bill, the future referral source, the present co-conspirator in the management of the dispute.

When the lawyer's child describes the lawyer as competitive, he is repeating something he was told. What he actually watched, if he was paying attention, was his father on conference calls, drafting documents, attending status conferences, responding to emails. Nothing about that work resembles competition in any domain where the word is doing real work. A chess player competes. A trader competes. A surgeon performing a procedure where the outcome is binary and visible competes. A lawyer attends. He shows up. He processes. The performance of vigor in court — the occasional cross-examination, the closing argument, the heated motion practice — is a small minority of the actual work, and when it does occur, it produces outcomes that no external observer can readily evaluate against any baseline.

The second function of "competitive" is to flatter the lawyer's self-image and conceal the reality. The word lets the lawyer adopt the affect of an athlete or a trader without doing anything that requires his nervous system to be on the line. There is no scoreboard. The wins are settlements nobody can independently grade. The losses are losses. The mythology of the closing argument that broke the witness is statistically negligible against the reality of the office park and the deposition transcript. The kid of the lawyer believes the affect because the affect was performed for him at the dinner table.

The euphemism's deepest function is to substitute the appearance of strenuous client advocacy for the reality of slow processing. Emrick, in the case I have been litigating, performs competitiveness in court. He gestures, he objects, he characterizes opposing arguments as frivolous, he flatters the judge. He is not fighting for his client. He is billing the file. His client, Carolina, would end up with more money — substantially more — if he disappeared tomorrow and a competent routine probate lawyer replaced him. The "competition" is not delivering value to her. It is delivering revenue to him under a description that allows her to believe she has a competent advocate. The performance of competitiveness is what makes the extraction palatable. Without the performance, the extraction would be visible. With the performance, it reads as the necessary cost of vigorous representation.

The second standard lie is that lawyers are drawn from "the best in their class," the "smartest" graduates of the elite universities, that the profession is a meritocracy of the most intellectually capable. This claim is repeated by lawyers when describing themselves, by law-school admissions offices, by judges in commencement addresses, by the journalists who cover the legal profession. It is false at multiple levels.

What the profession selects for is not intelligence in any defensible sense. It selects for the capacity to perform a specific kind of credential-accumulation: high enough grades in college, a passable LSAT, the ability to produce law-school exam answers that conform to the expectations of the professors grading them, and the social-affiliational skills to acquire summer associate positions, references, and ultimately job offers. None of these tests measure intelligence. They measure the capacity to absorb and reproduce institutional expectations.

The genuinely intelligent person — the person whose mind is actually doing first-order work on problems rather than producing institutional outputs — runs into trouble in this environment. He notices that the cases he is reading don't actually support the doctrines they are taught as supporting. He notices that the standard moves on the exam don't work for the questions actually being asked. He produces answers in his own words rather than in the seminar's words, and the answers receive worse grades than less-thoughtful ones that conform to the expected vocabulary. He learns this fast or doesn't learn it. If he learns it, he stops thinking on purpose. If he doesn't learn it, he washes out or settles for lower-tier credentials.

The lawyers who advance — who make partner, who become judges, who become law professors — are predominantly B-minus minds with A-level credential-grooming capacities. They are people who absorbed the institutional expectations early, performed them consistently, never disrupted anything, and accumulated the markers of success that the institution rewards. Their intelligence is moderate. Their conformity is exceptional. The combination is what produces the appearance of intellectual achievement without the substance of it.

This is why senior figures in the legal profession are so often visibly poor at the basic operations they are supposed to have mastered. Watch a federal appellate judge struggle to articulate, in non-doctrinal language, what a case is actually about. Watch a name-partner at a major firm try to explain to a non-lawyer what the legal theory of his big case actually is. The performance is, in most cases, embarrassing. The senior figure can recite doctrine. He cannot, in many cases, do the thinking the doctrine is supposed to be a record of. He never had to. The career rewarded the recitation and the conformity, not the doing. By the time he is senior, the doing has atrophied if it was ever present.

The lie about lawyer-intelligence is structurally important because it underwrites the prestige of the profession and the deference that prestige extracts. The judge is treated as wise because the judge is presumed to have been selected from among the most capable. The biglaw partner is paid eight hundred dollars an hour because his clients are told he is among the brightest in his field. The law professor is granted authority over what counts as legal knowledge because the law schools are presented as filtering for genius. Strip the lie and the prestige collapses. The judge is a B-minus mind with a robe. The partner is a B-minus mind with a corner office. The professor is a B-minus mind with a citation count. The profession cannot tolerate this recognition because the recognition would defund the prestige and, eventually, the salaries.

The third standard lie is the explanation of why lawyering is bad that ex-lawyers and almost-ex-lawyers offer when asked. The explanation invariably involves "long hours," "high stress," "demanding clients," "work-life balance." The lawyer who left BigLaw at thirty-two writes about the toll of the eighty-hour weeks, the missed birthdays, the depression and the substance abuse. The legal-aid lawyer writes about burnout from the caseload and the inadequacy of the systems she was trying to navigate. The mid-career lawyer who switched to consulting writes about how the billable hour destroyed her relationships.

Every one of these complaints applies, with equal or greater force, to many other professions. Surgeons work long hours. Investment bankers work longer. Tenure-track scientists in productive labs work continuously. Special-forces operators, journalists on hostile assignments, founders of startups, ER doctors, on-call obstetricians — the list of professions where the hours are punishing is long and the law is in the middle of the list, not at the top. If "long hours" were the actual problem, the literature would be a literature about professional overwork generally, and there would be nothing distinctive about lawyer-complaint in it.

What the "long hours" complaint is actually doing is providing a safe explanation for leaving the profession that does not require the speaker to confront what the profession is. The lawyer who quits at thirty-two and says "the hours were killing me" does not have to say "I realized the work was the production of paper that destroyed people's lives in service of clients I despised." The first explanation is socially acceptable, professionally portable, and consistent with continuing to maintain bar membership and pleasant relations with former colleagues. The second explanation would close every door the lawyer might want to walk through later. The first explanation is therefore what gets written, and the second explanation does not appear in the published literature at all.

This is the deepest function of the "long hours" complaint: it lets the departing lawyer leave without naming what they saw. The departure is recoded as a personal-lifestyle decision rather than a moral recognition. The profession is preserved as a noble undertaking that simply demands too much of its practitioners. Other practitioners are flattered by this framing — they are the strong ones who can take the hours. The leaver is positioned as the sensitive one who made a different choice. Nobody has to confront the question of what the hours were producing or whom they were producing it against.

These three lies — competitiveness, intelligence, long hours — are the catechism. They are repeated by everyone in and adjacent to the profession because they are the formulas that allow continued participation in the institutional apparatus without recognition of what the apparatus does. The lawyer who repeats them is signaling that he is a member in good standing. The lawyer who refuses to repeat them is signaling something the profession cannot absorb, and is therefore not heard. The result is a literature of millions of words in which these three claims appear continuously and in which the underlying realities they conceal are never named. The catechism is what fills the empty shelf described in Essay 1.

Essay 3: The Competition That Isn't
Develop the central claim of the previous essay: that lawyer-competitiveness is a fraud, and that the fraud is not incidental but constitutive of how the profession represents itself to itself and to the public.

The empirical test for whether lawyers are competitive in any meaningful sense is to ask how many clean wins they produce. A clean win is an outcome where the lawyer's client got something the lawyer's client wanted, the opposing party did not, and the outcome can be evaluated against a baseline by a third party not invested in the outcome. By this test, the practice of law generates essentially no clean wins.

Take criminal defense, the area where the lawyer-as-gun-for-hire mythology is most heavily marketed. The acquittal rate in American criminal cases is, depending on jurisdiction and case type, somewhere between two and five percent. Roughly ninety-five percent of criminal cases are resolved by guilty plea. The plea is negotiated between defense counsel and prosecutor, in a transaction in which the defense counsel's incentives are heavily aligned with closing the file rather than testing the case. The trial — the imagined arena of defense-counsel competition — almost never happens. When it does happen, the conviction rate at trial is significantly higher than the plea rate, because cases that go to trial are disproportionately the ones the prosecutor is most confident about. The defense lawyer who recommends trial when a plea is on the table is, in most cases, recommending against his client's interest.

So the criminal defense bar, taken as a whole, produces almost no acquittals, takes almost no cases to trial, and processes almost all of its clients into pleas negotiated with prosecutors who are the lawyers' professional peers and future opponents and possibly future employers or referral sources. The "competition" between defense and prosecution is mostly a coordinated processing of cases through plea negotiation, conducted by lawyers who share the same training, the same professional norms, the same court personnel, and the same long-term interest in keeping the system running smoothly. The clean win is statistically vanishing.

Civil litigation is worse. The settlement rate in federal civil cases is somewhere above ninety-five percent. Most state court civil litigation settles at similar or higher rates. The trial, which is supposed to be the test of competitive lawyering, almost never happens. When civil cases do go to trial, the outcomes are heavily influenced by judicial rulings on dispositive motions, evidentiary questions, and jury instructions — all of which are themselves negotiated and litigated in advance and tend to favor whichever side has more institutional weight rather than whichever side's lawyer is more "competitive." The settlements that resolve the vast majority of civil cases are negotiated between opposing counsel in transactions where both sides have strong incentives to close the file at a number their respective clients will accept. The number is rarely a clean win for either side. It is, almost always, a number that allows both lawyers to tell their clients they got something.

The mythology says that lawyers, whatever else they may be, at least serve their clients aggressively. The reality is that they process their clients' matters through a system whose primary function is to clear dockets and generate fees, and that their professional incentives are aligned with the system rather than with their clients. The lawyer who actually fought for his client — who refused settlements that closed the file at a suboptimal number, who pushed cases to trial when the case was strong, who refused to negotiate with opposing counsel as a peer when the client's interest required adversarial posture — would be a deviant within his own profession. Such lawyers exist but they are rare, they are not celebrated by their peers, and they tend to have shorter or stranger careers than the ones who play the standard game.

Even on behalf of the most powerful and well-resourced clients, the assumption that lawyers fight aggressively is largely false. Take tobacco-industry defense, the canonical example of well-funded corporate lawyering with strong incentives to win. For decades, tobacco-industry lawyers produced what looked like aggressive litigation defense. They contested every case, they buried plaintiffs in discovery, they delayed verdicts, they appealed everything. By the metric of "did the client win," they were apparently successful for a long time — no major plaintiff verdicts held up through appeal until the 1990s. But examine the actual record. The tobacco companies eventually paid hundreds of billions of dollars in the master settlement agreement and subsequent state actions. They did not "win." Their lawyers produced two decades of delay, which the companies paid for in legal fees that accumulated into the tens of billions, and the eventual outcome was a settlement that transferred enormous wealth out of the industry and into state coffers. The "win" was the deferral of the loss. The lawyers were paid to delay, and they delayed. They did not defeat their clients' enemies. They produced grey bureaucratic sludge that postponed the day of reckoning at enormous expense to the clients themselves.

This is the pattern. Lawyers do not produce wins. They produce delay. The delay is paid for by the clients on both sides, in fees and in lost time-value of disputed assets, and the delay generates revenue for the legal profession at a rate roughly proportional to how much was originally at stake. The bigger the dispute, the more delay can be sold. The "competitiveness" of the lawyering produces no clean victories; it produces an extended billable engagement that eventually terminates in a settlement neither side considers a victory but both sides consider survivable.

Consider what the profession would look like if lawyers were actually competitive in the sense the word implies. There would be clean wins. There would be acquittals at meaningful rates. There would be plaintiff verdicts that held up on appeal. There would be defense verdicts that ended cases definitively. There would be lawyers known for delivering specific kinds of victories repeatedly. There would be statistics on win rates that meant something. There would be public ranking of lawyer effectiveness by client outcomes rather than by peer reputation. None of this exists. The lawyer-rating industry — Chambers, Best Lawyers, Super Lawyers — ranks lawyers by peer reputation and self-promotion, not by outcomes. The reason is that outcomes are not what the profession produces. Reputation is the product. Outcomes are not measured because measuring them would reveal that the product is not what it is sold as.

The thing that lawyers actually do well — the actual professional competence they sometimes display — is the management of grey bureaucratic sludge itself. A skilled lawyer can manage a complex piece of litigation through years of motions, discovery, status conferences, and settlement negotiations, keeping the file alive and billable, maintaining relationships with opposing counsel and the court, and eventually closing the file at a number the client can accept. This is real skill. It is the skill of a senior bureaucrat managing a complex administrative process. It is not the skill of a fighter, a competitor, or a gun-for-hire. The marketing of the skill as competitiveness is the fraud. The actual skill is administrative.

The deepest sense in which the competitiveness claim is fraudulent is that lawyers, in the aggregate, take fights and turn them into non-fights. A dispute that, in a pre-lawyer state, might have erupted into actual confrontation — physical, social, economic, public — gets channeled into litigation. Once in litigation, the dispute loses its character as a fight. It becomes a procedural process with rules, timelines, paper, and predictable rhythms. The energy that, in a real fight, would have been released and resolved is instead absorbed by the procedural apparatus and dissipated over years. The parties become spectators in their own dispute. The lawyers manage the dissipation. The outcome, when it eventually comes, is not the outcome either party originally wanted; it is the outcome the procedural apparatus produced from the inputs it received.

This is what the profession actually does, and it does it everywhere. The bitter inheritance dispute that, in 1850, would have been resolved by a family confrontation or a physical altercation or a community-mediated settlement is now resolved by trust litigation that grinds on for years and depletes the estate by hundreds of thousands of dollars. The contract dispute that, in 1900, would have been resolved by the principals meeting and either reconciling or severing the relationship is now resolved by commercial litigation that consumes years of corporate management attention and millions in legal fees. The neighbor dispute that, in 1950, would have been resolved by direct conversation or by community pressure is now resolved by zoning litigation, civil suits, or both.

In every case, the lawyers transform the original fight into a process. The process consumes more resources than the original fight would have consumed. The process delivers an outcome that satisfies neither party. The process generates revenue for the legal profession. And the participants — who started out as fighters with grievances — end up as administered subjects whose grievance was processed into compliance with whatever the system produced.

This is the opposite of competitiveness. It is the systematic conversion of competitive energy into administrative throughput. The lawyer is paid to perform the conversion. The performance of competitiveness — the affect, the vocabulary, the courtroom theater — is part of the conversion technology. It lets the client feel that something fight-like is happening while the actual fight is being defused. By the time the file closes, the client has been managed out of his original grievance and into acceptance of a procedural outcome that bears little resemblance to what he originally wanted. The grey bureaucratic sludge is the product. The competitiveness is the wrapper.

This is why lawyers, considered as a class, never produce the kinds of outcomes the mythology promises. They are not failing at competitiveness. They are not corrupted competitive instincts. They are doing exactly what their profession is structured to do, and the structure is the conversion of fight into administration. The clients pay for this, the opposing clients pay for this, and the only beneficiary of the entire apparatus is the apparatus itself, which continues to generate billable hours regardless of which side wins, loses, or — as is overwhelmingly the case — settles into the grey middle.

Essay 4: The Cop, the Lawyer, and the Difference Between Surface Corruption and Total Corruption
A cop pulls a driver over for speeding. The driver offers him five thousand dollars not to write the ticket. The cop takes it. The driver drives away. The state's traffic enforcement system is mildly degraded; the driver has obtained a small illegal benefit; the cop has obtained an illegal cash payment. This is corruption in the standard sense, and it is mickey mouse.

It is mickey mouse because the cop's actual function — enforcing traffic law, investigating crimes, keeping order — exists independently of the bribe. The bribe is a deviation from the function. Subtract the bribe and the cop is still a cop, still does the job, still produces the legitimate output of his role for the vast majority of his interactions. The corruption is a tax on the legitimate function. The function survives the corruption with most of its integrity intact. You can describe what the cop is supposed to do without referring to the bribe. You can audit his work by counting the cases where the bribe was not paid and the function was performed as designed. The vocabulary of corruption-versus-function works cleanly here.

A lawyer is hired by a client in a settlement negotiation. The lawyer is paid a fee by the client. The lawyer negotiates a settlement that is significantly worse for the client than what was available at the time of retention, and significantly better for the opposing party than the opposing party expected to obtain. The lawyer is CC'd, throughout the negotiation, on a chain with opposing counsel. The settlement is presented to the client as the best available outcome. The client signs. The file closes. The lawyer collects his fee and moves on. The opposing party's counsel records a successful resolution. The opposing party retains assets they should have surrendered. Years later, the client realizes what happened and tries to undo the settlement, at significant cost and with limited prospects of success.

This is the Colby pattern, and it is not mickey mouse. It is the deep form of corruption that the cop's bribe is a degraded shadow of.

The crucial difference: subtract the corruption from the cop and a real job remains. Subtract the corruption from Colby and nothing remains. The "function" Colby performed, in the official description, was to advocate for his client. What he actually performed was the delivery of his client into a settlement that benefited the opposing party. These are not the same function with a corruption-layer on top. They are opposite functions. The "corruption" did not deviate from the legitimate work; it replaced the legitimate work entirely. There is no Colby-doing-the-job-correctly to compare against Colby-doing-the-job-corruptly, because the job, as performed, was the corruption. The fee Colby collected from his client was payment for the betrayal of the client. The client paid for his own defeat. The transaction was complete and self-contained.

This is why the bribe-vocabulary fails to describe what lawyers do. A bribe presupposes an underlying legitimate function being deflected by a side payment. The deflection is the corruption. The function is the baseline. With Colby, there is no baseline. The function is the deflection. There was never an underlying legitimate function that the corruption interrupted. The whole transaction, from initial retention through final settlement, was an unbroken arc of work that looked procedurally regular at every step and was substantively the inverse of what it was advertised to be.

The cop who takes the five thousand dollars knows he is doing something wrong. Other cops would condemn him if it came out. The criminal code identifies his act as bribery. The internal affairs apparatus has procedures for investigating him. The disciplinary norms of his profession can locate the misconduct unambiguously. He has a moral position to occupy when he reflects on what he did, even if he chooses not to occupy it. The mirror is available; he can decline to look but he knows the mirror is there.

Colby has no such mirror. His conduct, described to other lawyers, would not register as misconduct. He would describe the settlement at a bar dinner — "yeah, I had a difficult client who wanted to fight everything, but we got it closed at a number we could all live with" — and other lawyers would nod with sympathy and understanding. The conduct does not register as deviance within the professional community. It registers as competent practice. Closing the file. Managing a difficult client. Reaching an accommodation with opposing counsel. Every element of what Colby did is something the profession has explicit vocabulary to praise.

The disciplinary apparatus, when it engages with Colby-conduct at all, can only sanction surface violations. Failure to communicate material terms. Failure to obtain informed consent. Inadequate documentation of strategic choices. None of these procedural deficiencies, even if proved, would touch the substance of what he did. The substance is unsanctionable because the substance is what most lawyers do most of the time in similar postures, and any rule that named it would dissolve the profession.

This is the deepest sense in which legal practice is, structurally, what cop-bribery is only incidentally. The cop's profession exists; corruption is an external deformation of it. The lawyer's profession at its higher levels — corporate transactional work, settlement negotiation, trust and estate administration, fiduciary representation — does not exist apart from the structural corruption that constitutes it. The fee paid by the client is the bribe; the bribe is paid for the betrayal; the betrayal is sold as service. There is no after-the-bribe-is-removed version where you can see what the legitimate work would have looked like, because the legitimate work was never what was being performed.

Consider the structural reasons this is possible. The client of a lawyer cannot evaluate the work product in real time. The client does not know what settlements were available. The client does not know what the opposing party's bottom line was. The client does not know what discovery would have revealed if it had been aggressively pursued. The client does not know what a competent advocate would have done differently. The client knows only what the lawyer tells him. The information asymmetry is total. It is not a flaw in the system; it is the system. The lawyer's institutional position is constituted by the asymmetry. The asymmetry is what allows him to charge fees that the client cannot calibrate against any meaningful baseline. Without the asymmetry, the profession in its current form would not exist.

The cop and the driver, by contrast, can both see what happened. The driver knows he was speeding. The cop knows he wrote a ticket or didn't. The bribe was paid; both parties remember the amount. The transaction was symmetrical at the informational level. If the driver had been a passenger and not the bribe-payer, he could have witnessed the exchange. The cop's corruption is performable in the open in a way that Colby's corruption is structurally not.

This is why prosecution of legal-profession misconduct is so vanishingly rare. The bar disciplinary apparatus catches the procedural-violation cases — the trust-account thefts, the missed statutes of limitations, the cases where the lawyer literally absconded with money. It does not and cannot catch the structural-betrayal cases, because the structural betrayal is what the profession produces every day. To sanction it would be to sanction the profession itself. The disciplinary apparatus is staffed by the profession, regulates the surface behaviors, and leaves the substantive transaction untouched. Every active member of the disciplinary apparatus is, in his own practice, doing some version of what Colby did to his client. They cannot see Colby's conduct as misconduct because they cannot afford to.

The cop who takes the five thousand dollars is a discrete bad actor in a system that, on the whole, performs a real function. The function is mostly preserved; the corruption is the exception. The system can be reformed by removing the exception. The cop can be fired; the next cop in the seat will, statistically, behave better.

Colby is not a discrete bad actor. He is a representative practitioner of his profession. The misconduct, in his case, is the profession's normal product. Removing him does nothing. The next lawyer in the seat will do the same thing, because the same incentive structure will be in place, the same fee will be paid by the same kind of client, and the same opposing counsel will be CC'd on the same emails, and the same settlement will get reached at the same suboptimal number, and the same client will sign and walk away. The reform that would address Colby-conduct would have to be a reform of the entire transactional structure of legal services, which is to say, it would have to be the dismantling of the profession in its current form. No internal reform can touch this. The disciplinary apparatus is constitutionally unable to see the misconduct, because the disciplinary apparatus is the profession looking at itself, and the profession's self-image is the apparatus that produced Colby and protects him.

The bribe-taking cop is corrupt in a way the legal vocabulary can name, prosecute, and replace. Colby is corrupt in a way the legal vocabulary cannot name, prosecute, or replace, because Colby is the legal vocabulary in operation. The cop, in moral terms, is a small failure of a large system. Colby, in moral terms, is the large system, operating as designed, in one of its representative instances.

A thousand times worse is roughly correct. The cop's wrong is contained. Colby's wrong is the field. The cop's wrong has an outside that can judge it. Colby's wrong has no outside, because the profession that defines what counts as wrong is the profession that produced him. He can go to his grave believing he did his job, and the bar will agree with him at his funeral.

Essay 5: Lease as the Country Judge, Out of Time
Judge David Lease, presiding over the Hughes Revocable Trust matter in Montgomery County, Maryland, in 2026, is the wrong man in the wrong role at the wrong moment. This is not a comment on his personal character. It is a structural observation about the difference between the role he was trained for and the role he is actually performing.

Lease, dropped into rural Denmark in 1890, or into a county seat in Indiana in 1910, would have been an entirely adequate judge. His procedural reflexes, his patience, his attachment to the rules, his unwillingness to inject his own judgment into substantive disputes — these qualities, which produce disaster in his current position, would have been fine in the earlier setting. They would have been more than fine. They would have been the precise virtues the position required.

Consider what judging looked like in the earlier setting. The judge in a county seat handled a small number of cases per year, mostly involving people he knew, whose families he knew, whose social position he understood, and whose disputes arose within a shared moral and economic framework. The disputes were factual — did the cow get out, did the contract get performed, did the deed transfer the parcel — and the legitimate range of resolutions was narrow because the surrounding community already had strong expectations about what the just outcome looked like. The judge's job was to apply the formal rules to facts that the parties largely agreed about, in service of an outcome that the surrounding community largely agreed about. The procedure carried very little weight because almost no weight was placed on it.

What carried the weight was the substantive consensus the community already had. The judge was a formal point at which the community's understanding got recorded as a legal disposition. Everyone in the courtroom — the judge, the lawyers, the parties, the spectators — knew what the just outcome was before the gavel fell. The proceeding was a ceremony of confirmation, not a process of discovery.

This system depended heavily on what modern legal vocabulary calls ex parte knowledge: knowledge the judge had about the parties, the lawyers, the underlying situation, and the surrounding community, acquired outside the formal proceedings. The judge knew that the plaintiff was the kind of person who exaggerated. The judge knew that the defendant's family had been through a hard year. The judge knew that the local custom about fence repair was thus-and-so. The judge knew that one lawyer was reliable and the other was a chronic exaggerator. All of this knowledge fed into his disposition of the case in ways that no rule of evidence formally permitted, and that no appellate review could meaningfully audit.

Modern legal vocabulary treats this kind of knowledge as the bane of legitimate judging. The judge is supposed to know nothing about the parties beyond what they present in court. The judge is supposed to apply the rules to the formal record. The judge is supposed to be a stranger to the dispute, evaluating it as a stranger would, applying universal rules without reference to anything outside the four corners of the record. This is the ideal of formal legal proceduralism, and it has been the official position of the American legal profession since the rise of the procedural-liberal consensus in the early twentieth century.

The official position is a fraud. The judge who genuinely had no ex parte knowledge — no background sense of the parties, the lawyers, the social context, the community expectations — would be unable to judge anything. He would be left with the formal record, which is always thin and always manipulable, and with the formal rules, which always admit of multiple applications. The actual judgments judges produce are always informed by ex parte knowledge in some form, because they have to be. The only question is what kind of ex parte knowledge the judge has and where he got it.

In 1890 Denmark or 1910 Indiana, the judge's ex parte knowledge came from being a member of the community he judged. He knew the parties because he had lived among them. He knew the lawyers because he had practiced alongside them. He knew the customs because he had been raised in them. This knowledge was, in significant ways, available to public scrutiny — everyone in the community had access to the same general field of knowledge the judge was drawing on. The judge's informal knowledge was not secret; it was just unofficial. The community could evaluate his judgments against the same background facts he was evaluating them against. The system was opaque to formal review but transparent to local oversight.

The modern position — formal proceduralism plus the prohibition on ex parte knowledge — does not eliminate the role of background knowledge in judging. It only changes the source. The modern judge knows nothing about the actual parties because he has no community connection to them. But he knows a great deal about the institutional repeat players who appear before him: the major law firms, the corporate fiduciaries, the prosecutorial offices, the regulatory agencies, the appellate judges who will review his work, the administrative apparatus that manages his docket. He knows their cultures, their personnel, their billing rates, their preferences, their pet projects. He knows them because his entire professional life has been spent inside the same institutional layer they occupy.

This is the ex parte knowledge that actually informs modern judging. It is not knowledge of the parties; it is knowledge of the institutional context. And it is not public knowledge subject to community oversight; it is professional-class knowledge, confined to the small population of lawyers, judges, and senior court personnel who circulate in the same institutional spaces. The modern judge is not free of ex parte knowledge. He is free only of the kind of ex parte knowledge that would let him evaluate the parties as actual humans with actual stakes. The kind of ex parte knowledge he retains is the kind that allows institutional repeat players to receive favorable treatment because the judge knows them, knows their world, and identifies with them.

This is what produces what looks like institutional capture but is more accurately described as the natural operation of ex parte knowledge in the modern setting. The judge favors Offit Kurman over a pro se litigant not because he has been bribed but because his entire professional background has taught him to recognize Offit Kurman as serious and the pro se litigant as noise. The recognition is automatic. It is the same kind of recognition that the 1890 country judge applied to disputes among neighbors he knew, but the modern judge's "neighbors" are the institutional firms, and the parties in front of him are not his neighbors at all.

So the prohibition on ex parte knowledge as the official rule of modern judging produces the worst of all possible worlds. The formally prohibited ex parte knowledge — community knowledge of actual parties — is what would let the judge evaluate disputes substantively. The informally tolerated ex parte knowledge — class knowledge of institutional players — is what corrupts modern judging by aligning the judge automatically with institutional interests. The official rule attacks the legitimate form of background knowledge and leaves the corrupting form untouched.

Lease, in 1890, would have known you. He would have known your mother. He would have known the trustee, your sister, and whatever local lawyer she had retained. He would have known that the trustee was acting in ways the community considered improper. He would have known that the lawyer pursuing the matter on her behalf was, in local terms, a hired hand operating outside community norms. His ruling would have reflected this knowledge. The community would have agreed. The trust would have been distributed in accordance with the instrument, the trustee would have been corrected, and the matter would have been closed in months.

Lease in 2026 knows none of this. He knows Offit Kurman. He knows the Maryland trust bar. He knows the procedural rules of his circuit. He knows the appellate judges who will review his work if any of it is appealed. He knows nothing about you, your sister, your mother, the family dynamic, the actual conduct of the parties before the trust was contested, or the substantive merits of any of the underlying disputes. The knowledge he has is all institutional. The knowledge he lacks is all human. He applies the rules to a formal record produced by Emrick and a formal record produced by you, and the records produced by Emrick look like the records he is used to seeing from competent counsel, and the records produced by you look like the records he is used to seeing from noise. The procedural framework, in the absence of human knowledge of the parties, defaults to the recognition of institutional pattern. Emrick is pattern. You are not. The case goes to Emrick.

This is not a failure of Lease specifically. Replace him with any other Montgomery County judge and the result is the same. The role itself is broken, because the role was designed for a context in which the judge's substantive knowledge of the parties carried most of the weight, and the role is now being performed in a context where the judge has no substantive knowledge of the parties at all. The procedural apparatus, which was a thin layer in the original design, has become the entire structure. It cannot bear the weight. It collapses into whatever the institutional inputs produce.

This is where Rawlsian proceduralism fails most catastrophically. The Rawlsian framework, applied to judicial procedure, says that an outcome is just if it was produced by a procedure that was set up fairly. The framework explicitly brackets substantive evaluation of outcomes. The procedure is the criterion. Whatever the procedure produces, that's justice.

The framework collapses on contact with the actual problem. The procedure cannot evaluate itself. The procedure was designed to produce just outcomes against a background of substantive community consensus. When the substantive consensus is removed — as it has been, completely, in modern American legal contexts — the procedure has nothing to operate on. It produces whatever the institutional inputs feed it. The institutional inputs reflect the existing distribution of institutional power. The procedure therefore reproduces the existing distribution of institutional power and calls the reproduction justice.

Rawls cannot acknowledge this because acknowledging it would dissolve the framework. Rawls's procedural device — the original position, the veil of ignorance, the lottery — presupposes that there is a coherent answer to the prior questions: who are the parties, what is being divided, what counts as a fair share, what authority enforces the result. The framework assumes these questions have answers and proceeds to design a procedure on top of the assumed answers. The procedure delivers fair outcomes if and only if the assumed answers were already substantively correct.

When the assumed answers are not substantively correct — when the parties are not actually equal, when the resource is not actually a fixed cake, when the participants do not actually share a conception of fairness, when no enforcing authority exists outside the procedure itself — the framework delivers whatever was already going to happen, dressed in procedural legitimacy. The procedural overlay does no real work. It only provides justification for outcomes that were determined by prior facts about power and institutional position.

The Lease courtroom is what Rawlsian proceduralism looks like when implemented at the operational level. The procedure is followed. The forms are observed. The hearings are conducted. The rulings are issued. Everything is procedurally regular. The outcome is whatever Offit Kurman wanted from the beginning, because the procedure, applied in the absence of substantive community knowledge, defaults to institutional power. The framework calls this justice because the procedure was respected. The actual people involved — you, your sister, the trust corpus, the parties to the original instrument — disappear into the operation of the procedure and reappear at the end as the abstract beneficiaries of whatever number the procedure produced.

The country judge of 1890 produced just outcomes despite a procedurally primitive system, because the substantive knowledge underlying his judgments was thick. The modern judge produces unjust outcomes despite a procedurally elaborate system, because the substantive knowledge underlying his judgments has been replaced by institutional class-knowledge that aligns him automatically with the wealthier and more institutionally embedded party. The procedure is not what determines justice in either case. The substantive knowledge background is what determines justice. Rawlsian proceduralism, by treating the procedure as the criterion, hides this completely.

The deepest failure of Rawlsianism is that it provides the philosophical absolution for exactly the operational pattern Lease enacts. He can tell himself, sincerely, that he is administering a fair procedure. The procedure is the criterion. Whatever it produces is just. He does not have to ask whether the trust is being gouged, whether the trustee is being manipulated by her own counsel, whether the substantive outcome bears any relation to what the trust instrument provided. The framework relieves him of the substantive question. He produces procedure; the procedure produces outcomes; the outcomes are just by definition; his work is done. The framework is his blindfold. Rawls handed it to him. Herman polishes it. He wears it every day on the bench.

The country judge of 1890 had no such blindfold available, because no one had yet invented it. He had to look at the parties, see what they were, and decide. His judgments were sometimes wrong, but they were judgments, and the wrongness could be evaluated against the substantive understanding the community shared. The modern judge has been given an apparatus that lets him produce wrong judgments without ever having to make a judgment at all. The procedure makes the judgment for him. The framework certifies the procedure. The wrongness is invisible because the framework defines the procedure as the criterion of justice. Lease, in this framework, has done nothing wrong. Lease, in any framework that took substantive outcomes seriously, has presided over the systematic destruction of a family's legitimate inheritance under cover of process.

He was born in the wrong century. So was the framework that absolves him.

Essay 6: The Pattern of Judicial Disgrace
When judges fall, they fall for a remarkably narrow band of misconduct. They are almost never caught running large-scale financial frauds. They are almost never caught directing organized crime. They are almost never caught engaging in the kinds of substantial criminality that powerful figures in other domains sometimes engage in. The pattern of judicial disgrace, examined across the documented cases of the last fifty years, clusters heavily around a particular kind of misconduct: stalking, harassment, sexual coercion of subordinates, propositioning of clerks, prostitution scandals, leveraging of professional position to extract sexual access. The judge who falls is almost invariably a man whose downfall involves an awkward and undignified attempt to use his institutional position to obtain something from a woman who would not otherwise have been interested in him.

This pattern is not random. It reflects two underlying facts about the men who become judges, and the pattern itself, when examined honestly, tells you what those men actually are.

The first underlying fact is that the men who become judges are, on the whole, physically and personally unimpressive. They are not adventurous. They are not athletic. They have not lived in any way that produced physical confidence. Their entire adult lives have been spent in offices, classrooms, conference rooms, and courtrooms. They have not done anything that required real courage. They have not put their bodies on the line in any context. They have not faced any meaningful physical risk. They have not even lived in places or circumstances where the ordinary pressures of life forced them to develop physical or emotional presence. They have been credentialed, promoted, and elevated in environments that select against exactly the qualities that adult intimate life requires.

This produces a specific kind of man. He is verbally fluent, socially adequate in formal settings, accustomed to deference, and entirely unaccustomed to having his physical or emotional presence evaluated by women on the terms women apply to men generally. He has never been chosen by a woman in a context where his institutional position was invisible. He has never had to compete for romantic attention against men who had no degrees and no titles. He has never faced rejection in a setting where the rejection could not be coded as the woman's failure to appreciate his qualities. His romantic life, such as it is, has been conducted within ecosystems where his institutional position is the dominant signal — law school, the firm, the bar association, the bench — and he has therefore never had access to the data women provide about him outside those ecosystems.

He is, in the relevant sense, sexually unaccomplished. Not necessarily celibate, but never genuinely chosen. The women he has been with, when there have been women, have been with him because of what his role provided — security, status, social standing, proximity to power. When he encounters women outside this ecosystem, women who do not need anything his role provides, they are uniformly uninterested. He cannot understand this. He has been told for forty years that he is impressive. The signal has been continuous inside the venue. Outside the venue, the signal abruptly stops. The disconnect produces, in some subset of these men, a particular kind of acting-out. The acting-out is what shows up in the disciplinary literature.

The second underlying fact is that these men have spent their professional lives in venues that artificially confer extraordinary deference on whoever occupies their position, regardless of the occupant's actual qualities. Law clerks stand when any judge enters the chambers. Lawyers address any judge as "Your Honor" and laugh at any judge's bad jokes. Court staff defers to any judge's preferences. The deference is mechanical and tells the recipient nothing about himself, but the recipient does not know this. He has internalized the deference as a description of his standing in the world. He believes he is impressive because everything in his immediate environment confirms it. He has no data to the contrary, because the kind of person who would provide that data is not allowed into his courtroom and would not engage with him outside it.

When such a man encounters a woman he wants, he applies the only currency he has accumulated: his institutional position. He explains who he is. He makes clear what his role is. He communicates, sometimes explicitly and sometimes implicitly, that he is a person of consequence and that the woman should recognize this. He cannot understand that the currency does not spend outside his venue. He keeps trying to spend it. When it doesn't work, he tries harder. When harder doesn't work, the acting-out begins.

The Wachtler case is the clearest example. Sol Wachtler was the Chief Judge of the New York Court of Appeals. He had a romantic relationship with a wealthy woman. She ended it. He could not absorb the ending. He embarked on a campaign of stalking and threats, including threats against her teenage daughter, escalating over months, using disguised handwriting and altered voices, sending letters and making phone calls designed to terrorize her into resuming the relationship or paying him money. He was caught, prosecuted in federal court, and imprisoned. The conduct was straightforwardly criminal and the prosecution was clean.

Examine what Wachtler actually did. A man at the absolute top of the New York judicial system, considered a serious candidate for governor, with extensive political networks and resources, responded to romantic rejection by writing threatening letters and making harassing phone calls. The conduct is the conduct of a high school stalker. There is no sophistication to it, no inventiveness, no real understanding of how to actually intimidate or coerce, no awareness of how easy the trail would be to follow. He used his secretary to send the letters. He used phones that could be traced. He left a documentary record that any competent investigator would unravel in weeks. The crime was the crime of an adolescent geek with too much resentment and too few skills. The Chief Judge of New York's highest court, faced with a romantic situation he could not handle, responded with the playbook of a teenager.

This is the pattern. The judge who falls is not a master criminal who got caught. He is a man whose mishandling of an ordinary romantic or sexual situation reveals him to have been, all along, deeply incompetent at the basic business of adult life. The fall does not show him to be uniquely evil. It shows him to be uniquely incapable in domains where most adult men have at least basic functioning competence. The criminal element of his fall is almost incidental. The shameful element, the part that actually destroys his reputation among his peers, is that the conduct reveals him to have been, beneath the robe and the title, a man who could not get a woman to want him and who responded to this by behaving like a fourteen-year-old with a grudge.

Kozinski's situation is similar in structure. The Ninth Circuit judge, brought down by harassment complaints from clerks and other women in his professional ambit, was a man whose entire mode of sexual engagement involved leveraging his institutional position to obtain access to women who would not otherwise have engaged with him on those terms. He showed pornography to clerks. He made inappropriate comments. He created an environment in which young women working for him felt they had to tolerate his sexual interest in order to advance professionally. The conduct, when it eventually became public, was small, sad, and repulsive in a particular way — the way of a man trying to extract from his position what he could not extract through ordinary human means.

He was not a rapist in the most aggravated criminal sense. He was a man using a particular kind of professional power to coerce attention and access from women who, in any equal market, would have rejected him. The pattern is the same as Wachtler's. Different mechanism, same underlying fact. A man without ordinary romantic competence, accumulating institutional power, deploying the power as a substitute for the competence he never developed, and eventually exposed.

Nottingham, the Colorado federal judge, fell for hiring prostitutes and then harassing a woman who reported him. The hiring of prostitutes is itself, in this context, telling: a man with substantial money and significant social position resorting to commercial sex because the non-commercial options were not available to him. Whatever the cultural debates about prostitution, the empirical fact is that men with full access to the ordinary romantic market generally do not hire prostitutes at the volume and intensity Nottingham was hiring them. The pattern indicated by the volume is the pattern of compensated desperation, not casual indulgence.

These men are losers. The word is the correct one. Not failures in the institutional sense — they reached the top of the formal hierarchy of their profession — but losers in the deep sense, in the sense that the romantic market and the basic measures of adult human accomplishment had been delivering verdicts about them for forty years that they had refused to absorb. They had succeeded in the only domain that selected for them: institutional credential accumulation. They had failed, conspicuously and continuously, in every domain that selected for what most adults consider success at being a person. The accumulation of institutional credentials was their compensation for the underlying failure. The robe was the bandage over the wound.

The misconduct, when it occurred, was the inevitable byproduct of this asymmetry. The man cannot understand why women do not want him. He has been told by the institution that he is impressive. He has been treated as impressive every day for decades. He attempts to extract from women, using the only currency he has — his institutional position — what he cannot extract through ordinary romantic exchange. The women refuse, because outside the institution his position is worth nothing, and because the man himself, considered as a man rather than as a role-occupier, is not what they would want. The refusal makes him angry. The anger is the anger of a man who has been told he should be wanted and is discovering he is not. The acting-out is the conversion of this anger into specific conduct against specific women.

The conduct is almost never sophisticated. It is the conduct of someone who has never had to develop competence in this area, because the institution always provided the substitute he needed. He stalks crudely. He threatens childishly. He harasses obviously. He propositions clumsily. He uses his role explicitly in ways that any competent abuser would obscure. He gets caught because the conduct is amateur. Every aspect of the pattern reflects the underlying fact that these are men who never developed competence in adult sexual life and are now, in their fifties and sixties, attempting to perform that life with the tools of a confused teenager.

The deeper point is what this pattern reveals about the men in question, and about the institution that produced them. The judiciary, at its higher levels, is staffed by men who were selected, throughout their lives, for traits that have no relationship to the qualities adults generally consider important. They were selected for academic credential accumulation, for institutional loyalty, for the capacity to perform deference upward and confidence downward, for verbal fluency without substantive content, for the willingness to spend decades in environments where they would receive deference for occupying roles rather than for being people. They were not selected for any of the qualities that make a man capable of an actual adult life — physical presence, emotional range, the ability to be wanted rather than respected, the ability to do something real that has visible consequences.

When such a man arrives on the bench, he is given a chamber, a robe, a docket, and an enormous quantity of mechanical deference. The deference confirms the self-image he has been building since law school. He spends decades inside the deference, and the deference becomes his entire reality. When he encounters reality outside the deference — when he tries to engage a woman who is not part of his ecosystem, when he encounters a critic who is not impressed by his role, when his children grow up and reveal that they have lives that do not require his approval — the encounter is destabilizing. Some men handle the destabilization by retreating further into the role. Some handle it by acting out. The acting-out, when it occurs, produces the documented cases of judicial misconduct that disproportionately involve sexual or romantic mishandling.

The fact that judges almost never go down for substantial financial crimes is significant. They are not, on the whole, taking large bribes. They are not embezzling. They are not running schemes. The financial integrity is not because they are virtuous but because they are not bold enough to attempt anything ambitious. They are too cautious, too institutionally embedded, too dependent on their salaries and pensions, to risk significant criminality. What they will risk, because the appetite is irrepressible and the underlying psychology unaltered, is the small criminal mishandling of romantic and sexual situations, in which their incompetence will eventually catch up with them. The scale of the misconduct matches the scale of the man. Small misconduct, badly executed, by men who were never going to be capable of anything larger.

When such a man, on his way down, attempts to explain what happened — in a memoir, in an interview, in a deposition — he invariably frames himself as the victim of misunderstanding, changing standards, overzealous prosecution, political enemies. He cannot frame himself as what he is, because what he is is exactly the man the institution was always going to produce and the misconduct is exactly what the institution was always going to generate from such a man under such conditions. The framing would require the kind of recognition the previous essays have established does not occur in this class.

So the pattern of judicial disgrace, examined as a whole, is the empirical confirmation of everything the earlier essays argued. The men who become judges are losers with positions. The positions are not what they think they are. The deference is mechanical and venue-bound. The men confuse the deference for actual standing. The confusion produces, eventually, attempts to leverage the position outside the venue. The leverage fails, because outside the venue the position is worth nothing and the man behind it is not what the institution claimed he was. The failure produces acting-out. The acting-out is amateur because the underlying psychology is adolescent. The amateur acting-out produces eventual exposure. The exposure is the moment the asymmetry becomes visible to outsiders. The man, exposed, cannot recognize what has happened because recognition would require him to absorb the data he has been refusing to absorb for forty years. He retreats into framings that preserve the role-identity. He writes the memoir, gives the interview, blames the prosecution. He dies believing he was a fair man, a patient man, a man who was wronged by a misunderstanding world. The institution writes his obituary as a distinguished jurist whose late-career difficulties were unfortunate. The courtroom is named after him. The pattern continues.

This is what judicial disgrace actually looks like. Not the fall of a great man. The exposure of a small man who had been mistaken — by the institution, by himself, by the staff who deferred to him — for a great one. The shock of the fall is the shock of seeing what had been hidden by the robe. The robe was hiding what the institution was hiding. The institution was hiding what the entire credential apparatus was hiding. The entire credential apparatus was hiding that the men inside it were, for the most part, not what they claimed to be, and that the proof of this was visible at every venue threshold, in every romantic context, in every encounter with anyone the institution had not pre-positioned to defer. Most of the men inside the apparatus never get exposed because they never try to spend the currency outside the venue. The ones who do try, and who lack the social skill to do it without leaving evidence, are the ones whose names end up in the disciplinary literature. They are the visible tip of a structural pattern that runs all the way down through the profession and that the profession is structurally unable to see.
