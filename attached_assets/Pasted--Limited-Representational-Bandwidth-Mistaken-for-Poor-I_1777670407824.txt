# Limited Representational Bandwidth Mistaken for Poor Impulse Control

We think of criminals as having "poor impulse control." We think of them as beasts—creatures of appetite who know what they should do but cannot stop themselves from doing what they want. This picture is almost entirely wrong. What is usually going on is not poor impulse control. It is an inability to think about the non-imminent future. And that inability is, in turn, a consequence of an inability to think abstractly.

I learned this as a philosophy professor. I taught logic in a fairly bubble-test-oriented way, and over years of doing this in front of large and varied groups of students, I came to see that there is a hard divide—not a continuum—between people who can manipulate hypothetical content and people who cannot. The ones who could were not necessarily brilliant. They could just do it, instinctively, the way one walks. The ones who could not, could not at all. And the ones who could not did not present as morons. They were often sharp in their way: alert to the room, alert to who was lying, alert to what was happening around them. What they could not do was operate on content that was not in front of them. They could not run a syllogism. They could not entertain a hypothesis as a hypothesis. They could not reason from premises they did not currently believe.

The relevant fact is this: there is no way to think about the non-imminent future except through abstraction. The future is not perceptually given. The dog growling at you is given. Next week is not. To represent next week, you have to construct a hypothetical scenario and let it influence your present behavior, and that is an abstract operation. So a person who cannot reason abstractly is, automatically and as a matter of cognitive necessity, a person whose future is unavailable. Such a person is not weighing the future against the present and choosing the present. The future is not on the menu. From inside that representational range, what looks to outside observers like arch hedonism or impulsivity is locally rational—rational with respect to the contracted temporal domain over which utilities are actually being computed. The hedonism is an artifact of the contraction. Take the future away and what remains is not a beast. It is a creature whose incentive structure has been silently rewritten by its own cognitive limits.

Brain science confirms this. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, together with the anterior cingulate, is the region that supports both abstract relational reasoning and prospective thought. These are not two functions that happen to share an address. They are the same operation: holding in mind content that is not perceptually present and letting it influence current behavior. When this circuit is underpowered, both capacities fail together, and they fail in a characteristic way—steep delay discounting, weak hypothetical reasoning, behavior dominated by the situational present. When it is overpowered, you get the inverse syndrome: OCD, in which non-present threats cannot be dismissed and "what if?" never shuts off. Impulsivity and compulsion look like opposites behaviorally. They are the same dial set to opposite extremes.

This brings us to Bigger Thomas. Richard Wright wrote *Native Son* against the tradition of the noble suffering Black protagonist. He had said his earlier book had been too easy to weep over, and he resolved that his next protagonist would be one whose oppression had actually deformed him, not one whose dignity survived the deformation as a moral consolation to white readers. Bigger is dull, sullen, frightened, and capable of horrific violence: he kills Mary Dalton in panic and Bessie in cold calculation. He is the cognitive phenotype this analysis describes. His representational range is contracted. His future is not vivid. His behavior is governed almost entirely by the situational present. He is not cruel in the theatrical sense—he is not a villain enjoying the suffering of his victims. The thing that would have made the consequences real to him in advance is simply not running. Wright understood this and built the novel around it. Max's closing speech is a request that the reader understand Bigger, not that the reader forgive him. These are different requests.

It has to be acknowledged that Bigger conforms to what whites who think ill of Blacks have always thought of Blacks, and not to what liberals think of Blacks. Baldwin made exactly this objection: by centering Bigger, Wright had accepted the terms of the racist indictment rather than refuted them. Wright's reply, implicit in the novel itself, was that racism produces real Biggers, and that pretending otherwise to protect the race's image was a lie that served nobody. Wright chose truth over rhetoric. The disagreement cannot be settled at the level at which it is usually conducted.

What can be said is that the etiological question and the practical question are different questions, and that conflating them produces bad policy in both directions. The etiology is, in the metaphysical sense, exculpatory: Bigger is not evil in the theatrical sense, because the cognitive machinery whose absence is the proximate cause of his violence is machinery whose absence he did not choose. But this exculpation does not entail that society must absorb the danger. A person who genuinely cannot represent consequences is, by that very fact, not deterrable. He is also, by that very fact, not safely at large. To understand the cause of a danger is not to be obligated to tolerate the danger. One can be genuinely sorry that Bigger had to steal to eat and still recognize that, from a public-safety standpoint, he has to go.

The deeper point is that the cognitive substrate of moral and legal categories is not what those categories pretend it is. We speak of "criminals" and "the mentally ill" as if these were distinct populations defined by distinct kinds of inner life, and we speak of "impulsivity" and "compulsion" as if these were opposites. They are not opposites. They are the two failure modes of a single circuit. The same anatomical fact that produces the obsessive checker and the obsessive hand-washer also produces, when miscalibrated in the other direction, the man who kills the shopkeeper for a loaf of bread. Neither is choosing in the way that the folk-psychological vocabulary of choice supposes. One is overrun by a future that will not subside. The other is unreachable by a future that will not arrive. Between these two failures lies the narrow band of normal cognition, in which the future is present enough to constrain action but not so present that it tyrannizes it, and within which the moralized vocabulary of virtue and vice retains whatever residual application it has.

Bigger Thomas was not a hugely sympathetic character. That is not a flaw in the novel. That is the novel's argument.
