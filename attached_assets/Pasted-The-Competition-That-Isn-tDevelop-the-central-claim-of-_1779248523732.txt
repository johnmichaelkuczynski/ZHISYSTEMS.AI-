The Competition That Isn'tDevelop the central claim of the previous essay: that lawyer-competitiveness is a fraud, and that the fraud is not incidental but constitutive of how the profession represents itself to itself and to the public.The empirical test for whether lawyers are competitive in any meaningful sense is to ask how many clean wins they produce. A clean win is an outcome where the lawyer's client got something the lawyer's client wanted, the opposing party did not, and the outcome can be evaluated against a baseline by a third party not invested in the outcome. By this test, the practice of law generates essentially no clean wins.Take criminal defense, the area where the lawyer-as-gun-for-hire mythology is most heavily marketed. The acquittal rate in American criminal cases is, depending on jurisdiction and case type, somewhere between two and five percent. Roughly ninety-five percent of criminal cases are resolved by guilty plea. The plea is negotiated between defense counsel and prosecutor, in a transaction in which the defense counsel's incentives are heavily aligned with closing the file rather than testing the case. The trial — the imagined arena of defense-counsel competition — almost never happens. When it does happen, the conviction rate at trial is significantly higher than the plea rate, because cases that go to trial are disproportionately the ones the prosecutor is most confident about. The defense lawyer who recommends trial when a plea is on the table is, in most cases, recommending against his client's interest.So the criminal defense bar, taken as a whole, produces almost no acquittals, takes almost no cases to trial, and processes almost all of its clients into pleas negotiated with prosecutors who are the lawyers' professional peers and future opponents and possibly future employers or referral sources. The "competition" between defense and prosecution is mostly a coordinated processing of cases through plea negotiation, conducted by lawyers who share the same training, the same professional norms, the same court personnel, and the same long-term interest in keeping the system running smoothly. The clean win is statistically vanishing.Civil litigation is worse. The settlement rate in federal civil cases is somewhere above ninety-five percent. Most state court civil litigation settles at similar or higher rates. The trial, which is supposed to be the test of competitive lawyering, almost never happens. When civil cases do go to trial, the outcomes are heavily influenced by judicial rulings on dispositive motions, evidentiary questions, and jury instructions — all of which are themselves negotiated and litigated in advance and tend to favor whichever side has more institutional weight rather than whichever side's lawyer is more "competitive." The settlements that resolve the vast majority of civil cases are negotiated between opposing counsel in transactions where both sides have strong incentives to close the file at a number their respective clients will accept. The number is rarely a clean win for either side. It is, almost always, a number that allows both lawyers to tell their clients they got something.The mythology says that lawyers, whatever else they may be, at least serve their clients aggressively. The reality is that they process their clients' matters through a system whose primary function is to clear dockets and generate fees, and that their professional incentives are aligned with the system rather than with their clients. The lawyer who actually fought for his client — who refused settlements that closed the file at a suboptimal number, who pushed cases to trial when the case was strong, who refused to negotiate with opposing counsel as a peer when the client's interest required adversarial posture — would be a deviant within his own profession. Such lawyers exist but they are rare, they are not celebrated by their peers, and they tend to have shorter or stranger careers than the ones who play the standard game.Even on behalf of the most powerful and well-resourced clients, the assumption that lawyers fight aggressively is largely false. Take tobacco-industry defense, the canonical example of well-funded corporate lawyering with strong incentives to win. For decades, tobacco-industry lawyers produced what looked like aggressive litigation defense. They contested every case, they buried plaintiffs in discovery, they delayed verdicts, they appealed everything. By the metric of "did the client win," they were apparently successful for a long time — no major plaintiff verdicts held up through appeal until the 1990s. But examine the actual record. The tobacco companies eventually paid hundreds of billions of dollars in the master settlement agreement and subsequent state actions. They did not "win." Their lawyers produced two decades of delay, which the companies paid for in legal fees that accumulated into the tens of billions, and the eventual outcome was a settlement that transferred enormous wealth out of the industry and into state coffers. The "win" was the deferral of the loss. The lawyers were paid to delay, and they delayed. They did not defeat their clients' enemies. They produced grey bureaucratic sludge that postponed the day of reckoning at enormous expense to the clients themselves.This is the pattern. Lawyers do not produce wins. They produce delay. The delay is paid for by the clients on both sides, in fees and in lost time-value of disputed assets, and the delay generates revenue for the legal profession at a rate roughly proportional to how much was originally at stake. The bigger the dispute, the more delay can be sold. The "competitiveness" of the lawyering produces no clean victories; it produces an extended billable engagement that eventually terminates in a settlement neither side considers a victory but both sides consider survivable.Consider what the profession would look like if lawyers were actually competitive in the sense the word implies. There would be clean wins. There would be acquittals at meaningful rates. There would be plaintiff verdicts that held up on appeal. There would be defense verdicts that ended cases definitively. There would be lawyers known for delivering specific kinds of victories repeatedly. There would be statistics on win rates that meant something. There would be public ranking of lawyer effectiveness by client outcomes rather than by peer reputation. None of this exists. The lawyer-rating industry — Chambers, Best Lawyers, Super Lawyers — ranks lawyers by peer reputation and self-promotion, not by outcomes. The reason is that outcomes are not what the profession produces. Reputation is the product. Outcomes are not measured because measuring them would reveal that the product is not what it is sold as.The thing that lawyers actually do well — the actual professional competence they sometimes display — is the management of grey bureaucratic sludge itself. A skilled lawyer can manage a complex piece of litigation through years of motions, discovery, status conferences, and settlement negotiations, keeping the file alive and billable, maintaining relationships with opposing counsel and the court, and eventually closing the file at a number the client can accept. This is real skill. It is the skill of a senior bureaucrat managing a complex administrative process. It is not the skill of a fighter, a competitor, or a gun-for-hire. The marketing of the skill as competitiveness is the fraud. The actual skill is administrative.The deepest sense in which the competitiveness claim is fraudulent is that lawyers, in the aggregate, take fights and turn them into non-fights. A dispute that, in a pre-lawyer state, might have erupted into actual confrontation — physical, social, economic, public — gets channeled into litigation. Once in litigation, the dispute loses its character as a fight. It becomes a procedural process with rules, timelines, paper, and predictable rhythms. The energy that, in a real fight, would have been released and resolved is instead absorbed by the procedural apparatus and dissipated over years. The parties become spectators in their own dispute. The lawyers manage the dissipation. The outcome, when it eventually comes, is not the outcome either party originally wanted; it is the outcome the procedural apparatus produced from the inputs it received.This is what the profession actually does, and it does it everywhere. The bitter inheritance dispute that, in 1850, would have been resolved by a family confrontation or a physical altercation or a community-mediated settlement is now resolved by trust litigation that grinds on for years and depletes the estate by hundreds of thousands of dollars. The contract dispute that, in 1900, would have been resolved by the principals meeting and either reconciling or severing the relationship is now resolved by commercial litigation that consumes years of corporate management attention and millions in legal fees. The neighbor dispute that, in 1950, would have been resolved by direct conversation or by community pressure is now resolved by zoning litigation, civil suits, or both.In every case, the lawyers transform the original fight into a process. The process consumes more resources than the original fight would have consumed. The process delivers an outcome that satisfies neither party. The process generates revenue for the legal profession. And the participants — who started out as fighters with grievances — end up as administered subjects whose grievance was processed into compliance with whatever the system produced.This is the opposite of competitiveness. It is the systematic conversion of competitive energy into administrative throughput. The lawyer is paid to perform the conversion. The performance of competitiveness — the affect, the vocabulary, the courtroom theater — is part of the conversion technology. It lets the client feel that something fight-like is happening while the actual fight is being defused. By the time the file closes, the client has been managed out of his original grievance and into acceptance of a procedural outcome that bears little resemblance to what he originally wanted. The grey bureaucratic sludge is the product. The competitiveness is the wrapper.This is why lawyers, considered as a class, never produce the kinds of outcomes the mythology promises. They are not failing at competitiveness. They are not corrupted competitive instincts. They are doing exactly what their profession is structured to do, and the structure is the conversion of fight into administration. The clients pay for this, the opposing clients pay for this, and the only beneficiary of the entire apparatus is the apparatus itself, which continues to generate billable hours regardless of which side wins, loses, or — as is overwhelmingly the case — settles into the grey middle.
