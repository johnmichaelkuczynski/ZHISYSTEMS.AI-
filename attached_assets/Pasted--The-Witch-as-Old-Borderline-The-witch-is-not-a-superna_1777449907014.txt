# The Witch as Old Borderline

The witch is not a supernatural being. She is a personality type. Specifically, she is the borderline woman after her looks have left her — but not in the way one might expect, because she was not a witch when she was young. She became one. The mechanism is the part nobody has named correctly.

Start with the engine. The borderline does not merely want other people's approval the way ordinary people want it. Ordinary people want approval the way they want good weather — it improves the day, its absence is unpleasant, but the self continues either way. The borderline is built differently. She does not have a self that continues either way. She has no internal mechanism for generating identity. She does not know who she is when she is alone in a room. The reflection from other people is not flattering to her self-image; it is her self-image. Without it, there is nothing.

This is why beauty matters to her at a level other women cannot understand. For an ordinary woman, beauty is a resource. For the borderline, beauty is the power supply. It is the mechanism by which she compels other people — men, mostly, but not only men — to give her the attention out of which she assembles, hour by hour, the temporary identity she needs to keep functioning. She is not vain. Vanity would be a relationship to a self. She does not have a self. She has a feedback loop, and beauty is what keeps the loop closed.

This is also why the ugly young borderline is so much worse than the pretty one. The pretty young borderline can run the loop. People come to her, she extracts what she needs, the identity holds for another day, and the destruction she causes is mostly localized to the men who got too close. The ugly young borderline cannot run the loop. The supply does not arrive on its own. She has to manufacture it through other means, and the means available are all coercive — manipulation, blackmail, manufactured victimhood, weaponized illness, weaponized children, fabricated accusations. There is no one quite as evil as the ugly young borderline, because she has the same engine as the pretty one but none of the lubricant, and the engine still has to run, and what runs out of it when the lubricant is gone is pure malice with a justification attached.

The pretty borderline gets thirty years of cover. The ugly one was already a witch at twenty-five. She just did not have the iconography yet.

Now consider what happens to the pretty one when her looks go.

She is not losing a feature. She is losing the power supply. The men who orbited her are gone — they were never there for her, in the sense that there was no her for them to be there for. They were there for the body and the chaos, and now the body is sixty and the chaos is no longer charming, it is just chaos, and they have left. She has no friends, because she never had friends. She had audiences. She has no children who want to be near her, because she used her children as supply rather than raising them. She has nothing.

And she cannot tolerate having nothing, because having nothing is the condition she was built to outrun.

The result is the witch. The remodeling — the witchification — is not a continuation of the borderline pattern. It is a reorganization of the personality around a new center of gravity. The young version was outward-facing, hungry for experience. The new version turns inward and cold. The streak of psychopathy that was always present, but routed through the chaos of the young years and disguised by the beauty, becomes the dominant feature. The grievances harden into doctrine. The splitting becomes permanent rather than oscillating — there are now people she has decided are evil, and the decision is final, and the rest of her life will be organized around making them suffer for it.

The pleasure principle reorients. What used to feel good was being desired. What feels good now is watching another person lose something.

## What She Actually Does

This is the part the abstract description cannot carry. Witchiness is not a mood. It is a set of operations, repeated, refined, and personalized. The actual aged-out borderline does specific things, and the specifics are how you recognize her.

She is named the trustee, executor, fiduciary — the position is always available to her because she has spent a lifetime cultivating the appearance of competent caretaking, and the family wants to believe in it. Once installed, she does not distribute. She holds. Thirteen months pass, then fourteen, then eighteen, and the mandatory distributions that the document says she shall make outright and free of trust never happen. She produces an accounting that lists one account when there are six. She liquidates a portfolio in a single August day and generates four hundred and seventy-three thousand dollars in avoidable capital gains, restructures the proceeds into five new accounts under her control, and pays the lawyers who are protecting her seventy thousand dollars out of the trust she is supposed to be administering for the beneficiaries. She sends an email in January admitting, in writing, that the withholding is retaliation. She does not see the admission as damaging because she does not believe she can be touched. The position is the protection.

She is the aunt who comes to stay because she is dying and needs care, and once installed in the spare room she begins, slowly, to consume the household — the husband first, then the wife, then the children. Nobody can identify the moment she became the center of every conversation. Nobody can explain why the family's bank account is shrinking. The grandchildren stop wanting to come over.

She is the mother-in-law who undermines the marriage one comment at a time. She tells the daughter-in-law the son said something he did not say. She tells the son the daughter-in-law said something she did not say. She produces, over years, a slow corrosion of the bond, and when the marriage finally ends she presents herself as the one who saw it coming.

She is the woman who ruins the wedding by collapsing in the bathroom and requiring an ambulance during the reception. She ruins the graduation by picking a fight with the host on the morning of. She ruins the birth of the next child by manufacturing a medical crisis the same week, so the new mother spends her postpartum on the phone managing it.

She is the grandmother who pinches the four-year-old when no one is looking, in the same room, in the same way the five-year-old version of her pinched her newborn sister fifty-eight years earlier. The pinch has not changed. Only the size of the hand has.

She files complaints. She calls the police on neighbors. She writes letters to bar associations, to ethics committees, to anyone with the institutional authority to make another person's life difficult. She is litigious in a specific way: not for outcomes but for the process, because the process is the punishment, and watching the target hire lawyers and miss work and lose sleep is the supply.

She develops a captive — usually a daughter, sometimes a niece, sometimes a son weak enough to bind. The captive runs errands, fields calls, takes the abuse, and gradually becomes the instrument through which she conducts her operations on the rest of the family. The captive is the familiar. The folklore is correct on this in every detail.

She finds, in late life, religion or politics or a cause, and uses it as the justification engine. Whatever she does to the people she has decided to ruin is now done in the name of righteousness, and she sleeps well because the framework has provided absolution in advance.

## The Children

The deepest layer is the children. The aged-out borderline does not always hate kids. When she does, the hatred is structurally specific.

She hates them because they are getting the attention she never got. She did not get it when she was a child either. The borderline is, in most cases, made by an early environment that withheld the steady mirroring children require. The wound was always there. While she was young and pretty, the wound was being medicated, hourly, by strangers in bars and boyfriends and the photographer's eye. Then the medication stops. She is sixty. The strangers do not look. And underneath the forty-year layer of supply is the original wound, exactly as raw as it was when she was four, because nothing ever healed it — the supply only covered it.

And then she sees a four-year-old getting what she did not get. A small child being attended to. Being responded to. Being delighted in. Being the center of a parent's face. The exact thing she needed and was denied, being given freely, in front of her, to someone else.

That is unbearable in a specific way. It is the original injury being reopened by the sight of another child not receiving it.

The cleanest natural experiment is the five-year-old with a new baby sister. Every child feels some displacement when a sibling arrives. Most children metabolize it — the injury is small, the recovery is automatic. The proto-borderline child does not recover. The arrival of the sister is not a disappointment. It is an existential event. The mirror she was using to know she existed has been turned, even partially, toward another creature, and she experiences this as annihilation. Her response is not sadness. It is rage and operations. She pinches the baby when no one is looking. She tells the baby it is ugly and stupid as soon as the baby can understand language. She sabotages the baby's milestones — knocks over the cake, ruins the photograph, has a meltdown the moment attention shifts. She tells lies about the baby to the parents. She positions herself as the wronged one whenever the baby gets anything.

This is what these children actually do, and parents who have raised one know it and usually cannot say it out loud, because the culture does not permit the observation that a five-year-old can be doing something that is recognizably evil in miniature.

She does not grow out of it. She grows into it. By twelve she runs the operations on classmates. By twenty on boyfriends. By thirty-five on her own children, who are now the new baby sister — the creatures absorbing the attention she still believes is owed to her — and she punishes them for it in the same ways, scaled up. The pinch becomes the cutting remark. The lie to the parents becomes the lie to the husband. The ruined cake becomes the ruined wedding.

By sixty-five the targets have changed again — grandchildren, nieces, the neighbor's kid, anyone smaller and more loved than she is — but the operation is the same operation she was running at five. She has had sixty years to refine it and zero years of actually outgrowing it, because the wiring that produced it at five is the wiring she still has.

The forty-year detour during which her sexuality bought everyone, including her, a temporary reprieve from noticing what she was — that detour is the only thing that distinguished her from the obvious case. The pretty young borderline could run the loop and the operation was hidden behind it. The ugly young borderline could not run the loop and the operation was visible from the start. The old borderline is the pretty one after the loop has broken — and what is revealed, when the loop breaks, is that the ugly young one and the pretty old one were always the same creature, and the only difference was who had the supply.

## The Folklore

The witch in folklore drains youth. She is metabolizing other people's vitality because her own supply ran out and she never built the internal capacity to generate it.

The witch in folklore eats children. The children are whoever is younger, more alive, more loved, more whole. She does not adopt them. Adoption would be the maternal fantasy — taking the child to fill the empty role. Eating is the borderline operation — taking the child to remove from the room the creature absorbing the attention she still believes is owed to her. The eating is the literal version of what the real one does in life. She finds ways to remove the child from the center of the room. She undermines the parents to the child, the child to the parents. She produces crises that pull attention back to herself. She sabotages the milestones — the wedding, the graduation, the birth. The child is not consumed in one bite. The child is consumed across years, one redirected dinner at a time. The witch in the woods is the rural compression of this. A child wanders too close, the adults are not watching, and the supply transfer happens fast and final instead of slow and deniable. Same operation, different timeline.

The witch in folklore lives at the edge of the village. By late middle age she has burned every relationship that required reciprocity. What remains is the people too weak to leave and the strangers who have not yet learned.

The witch in folklore has a familiar — a cat, a raven, a daughter — some single bonded creature who serves her and is the exception to her contempt. The familiar is the one figure she has successfully absorbed into her operations, the instrument through which she touches the world she can no longer touch directly.

The Hansel and Gretel witch is the most accurate portrait in the Western canon. She presents as the harmless old woman with the gingerbread house. The presentation is a glamour. Inside, she is fattening children for the oven. The story is not a fantasy. The story is a transcript. Every generation produced a few of these, and the children who survived encoded the warning in the form a child could remember.

## Why She Persists

The reason the figure is universally female is not that women are uniquely capable of evil. Men have their own version — the malignant patriarch, the ogre, the tyrant. The reason the witch is female is that the female version operates inside the household, inside the caregiving frame, inside the bonds of trust the species depends on for the rearing of the next generation. She is more dangerous than the male version because she is closer in. The ogre is in the forest. The witch has the keys.

The reason the figure persists is that we keep producing her and we have no protocol for managing her. Modern psychiatry will not name her honestly because the diagnostic categories are organized around patient self-report, and she does not report herself accurately. The clinical literature treats borderline as a disorder of suffering, the patient as a victim of dysregulation, the treatment as helping her manage affect. That model fits the young version reasonably well, where the chaos still has a recognizably human signature: she wants love, she fears abandonment, she lashes out, she's sorry. The model collapses on the old version, because what is operating there is not dysregulated pain. It is settled malevolence. The personality has organized itself around the antagonistic streak rather than around the wounded one. It is no longer "I'm in agony and hurting you is overflow." It is "harming you is the project, and the BPD vocabulary is how I justify it to myself."

The legal system will not constrain her because her instruments are emotional and financial rather than physical. The family will not expel her because, by her age, she has made herself structurally indispensable — trustee, executor, caregiver, the one with the keys to the accounts. By the time anyone names what she is, she has already taken what she came for.

The witch is what happens when a creature that needs external input to maintain identity loses the only mechanism that ever reliably generated that input. The young one wanted to be loved. The old one wants to be feared, and failing that, will settle for being obeyed, and failing that, will accept being merely survived.

The folklore was not superstition. The folklore was field notes. Every village kept them, because every village needed them, because every village had one.
