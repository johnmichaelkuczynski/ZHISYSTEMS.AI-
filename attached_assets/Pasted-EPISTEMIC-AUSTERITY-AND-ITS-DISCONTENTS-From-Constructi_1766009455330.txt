EPISTEMIC AUSTERITY AND ITS DISCONTENTS:
From Constructive Empiricism to the General Failure of Minimal-Commitment Epistemology

John-Michael Kuczynski, Ph.D.
Zhi Systems


ABSTRACT

This paper argues that constructive empiricism, as articulated by Bas van Fraassen, is not merely a flawed philosophy of science but a specific instance of a more general epistemological failure: the demand that rational belief not exceed what is strictly licensed by available evidence. I call this demand "minimal-commitment epistemology" or "epistemic austerity." The previous papers in this series demonstrated that constructive empiricism is retrospectively parasitic on realist cognition, provides no forward-looking guidance, and collapses into skepticism when pressed. This paper identifies the deeper structural reason for these failures: the austerity demand is self-defeating because cognition necessarily generates surplus—more commitment than evidence strictly warrants. The very concepts required to state the austerity demand (evidence, entitlement, adequacy, observation) are themselves instances of epistemic surplus. Constructive empiricism thus exemplifies a general pattern: positions that counsel restraint can only be articulated after surplus-generating cognition has already succeeded. They are, in the precise sense developed here, forms of epistemic rent-seeking—extracting normative authority from a practice while disavowing responsibility for the conditions that made the practice possible.


1. INTRODUCTION: THE DEEPER DIAGNOSIS

The preceding papers in this series have subjected constructive empiricism to sustained critique. "Constructive Empiricism as Retrospective Parasitism" argued that van Fraassen's program cannot describe, guide, or explain scientific practice, and that it presupposes the very realist commitments it officially disavows. "Epistemic Scolding as Philosophy of Science" argued that van Fraassen's self-understanding is mistaken: he thinks he is disciplining belief, but he is merely withholding epistemic honorifics from beliefs on which scientific practice depends.

Those critiques are correct as far as they go. But they do not identify the deepest structural reason why constructive empiricism fails. The purpose of this paper is to provide that diagnosis. The thesis is this: constructive empiricism fails because it is an instance of a more general epistemological impossibility—the demand that cognition operate at break-even, generating no commitment beyond what evidence strictly warrants.

I call this demand "epistemic austerity" or "minimal-commitment epistemology." The demand sounds prudent. It presents itself as intellectual discipline, as the refusal to overclaim. But the demand is incoherent. Cognition cannot operate at break-even. The very possibility of knowledge requires epistemic investment that exceeds strict entitlement. The concepts that articulate the austerity demand are themselves products of surplus. The demand can therefore only be stated after surplus-generating cognition has already succeeded—which means the demand is parasitic on the very mode of cognition it purports to restrain.

Once this structural point is clear, the specific failures of constructive empiricism become instances of a general pattern. Van Fraassen's program fails not because of local argumentative errors but because it instantiates an impossible epistemological ideal.


2. THE AUSTERITY DEMAND

Let us state the demand explicitly. Epistemic austerity holds that rational belief should not exceed what is strictly warranted by available evidence and valid inference. On this view, any commitment beyond the minimal—any epistemic surplus—represents a failure of discipline. The ideal cognitive agent believes only what must be believed, ventures no further than the data compel.

Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism is a paradigm case. The constructive empiricist accepts theories as empirically adequate—as correctly representing observable phenomena—but withholds belief in claims about unobservables. The justification is that belief in unobservables outruns what observation strictly licenses. We may need such beliefs for practical purposes, but we are not epistemically entitled to them.

The same structure appears throughout the history of epistemology. Humean skepticism about induction, Cartesian doubt about the external world, phenomenalist reductions of objects to sense-data, and verificationist constraints on meaning all share the austerity impulse: do not commit beyond what is strictly given; do not believe more than you must.

The motivation is understandable. Error is costly. Overcommitment is dangerous. Intellectual humility is a virtue. But the question is whether the demand can be coherently formulated, let alone satisfied.


3. WHY COGNITION REQUIRES SURPLUS

The austerity demand fails because cognition necessarily generates surplus. This is not a contingent psychological fact about human beings; it is a structural feature of what cognition is.

Consider what is involved in any genuine cognitive act—any episode of learning, predicting, explaining, or intervening. Such acts necessarily involve commitments that outrun what is strictly given in immediate experience.

Prediction requires projecting patterns from observed cases to unobserved ones. But no finite set of observations entails anything about unobserved cases. The move from "all observed emeralds are green" to "the next emerald will be green" involves commitment that exceeds strict entitlement. Cognition cannot proceed without such projections.

Explanation requires positing structure that is not directly observed. We explain the behavior of gases by appeal to molecules we do not see. We explain symptoms by appeal to pathogens we cannot directly perceive. Every explanatory inference commits us to more than observation strictly warrants.

Even basic cognitive operations presuppose object persistence—the continued existence of entities between observations. But persistence is never observed; only discrete states are. The belief that the coffee cup persists while I look away is an overcommitment relative to my evidence. Yet without such overcommitment, I cannot navigate the world at all.

Rational action requires evaluating counterfactuals: what would happen if I did this rather than that? But counterfactual reasoning commits us to claims about situations that, by definition, do not obtain. No amount of actual observation can strictly entitle us to such claims.

The conclusion is unavoidable: cognition operates above break-even. It must generate epistemic surplus—more structure than immediately observed, more commitment than strictly licensed—in order to function at all. If you try to run cognition at "minimal entitlement," you do not get safer cognition. You get no cognition.


4. THE MINIMAL IS ALREADY SURPLUS

The austerity advocate might reply: "I grant that ordinary cognition involves overcommitment. My point is precisely that we should be more disciplined." But this reply fails to appreciate the depth of the problem. The very concepts deployed in articulating the austerity demand are themselves instances of epistemic surplus.

Consider what the advocate of restraint must presuppose. She must presuppose the concept of evidence—but evidence is not given in raw experience; it is a theoretical posit involving assumptions about reliability, relevance, and the distinction between signal and noise. She must presuppose the concept of entitlement—but entitlement is a normative notion that goes beyond any particular observation. She must presuppose the concepts of belief and commitment—but these are theoretical constructs posited to explain and predict behavior.

Most fundamentally, she presupposes the concepts of object, cause, and law—the very concepts that constitute the framework within which evidence, entitlement, and belief can be distinguished. But these concepts are not extracted from sense data; they are imposed on sense data. They are the surplus that makes cognition possible.

This is where C.I. Lewis's insight becomes decisive. As argued in "Constructive Empiricism as Retrospective Parasitism," perceptual concepts are inherently modal. To call something a mouse is already to commit to counterfactuals about its behavior—it would flee from a cat, it would gnaw soft materials, it would reproduce in certain ways. To call something steel is to commit to claims about its tensile strength, its behavior under heat, its conductivity. None of this is reducible to sense-data descriptions. The modal content is essential to the concepts.

The demand to believe only what is strictly entailed by the evidence presupposes a conception of evidence that is itself not entailed by anything. The minimal product already exceeds the minimal.


5. CONSTRUCTIVE EMPIRICISM AS AUSTERITY INSTANCE

We are now in a position to see why constructive empiricism fails in exactly the ways the previous papers documented.

Why is constructive empiricism retrospectively parasitic? Because the austerity demand can only be articulated after surplus-generating cognition has already succeeded. Van Fraassen's program presupposes that scientists form hypotheses, posit causes, project regularities, and test predictions—all of which involve epistemic surplus. Only after this realist cognitive work has been done can the constructive empiricist step in and say: "Whatever you thought you were doing, you were only achieving empirical adequacy." The relabeling adds nothing because it depends on the very commitments it purports to restrain.

Why does constructive empiricism provide no forward-looking guidance? Because guidance requires surplus. To tell someone how to reason under uncertainty, when to trust a theory, or how to allocate epistemic risk, you must appeal to considerations that go beyond what evidence strictly warrants. Pragmatism and instrumentalism provide guidance precisely because they license epistemic investment. Constructive empiricism, committed to austerity, can only offer retrospective reinterpretation.

Why does constructive empiricism collapse into skepticism? Because the austerity demand, consistently applied, eliminates the grounds for any belief. Once you insist that belief not exceed strict entitlement, you discover that strict entitlement gives you nothing—not objects, not causes, not persistence, not regularities. The observable/unobservable distinction cannot save the position because observable-language is already theory-laden, already shot through with modal commitments that exceed phenomenal description.

Why is van Fraassen's self-understanding mistaken? Because he thinks he is disciplining belief-formation when he is merely withholding honorifics from beliefs that have already done their cognitive work. Genuine discipline would involve constraints on inference and commitment. Van Fraassen explicitly allows realist hypothesis formation, explanation, intervention, projection, and vocabulary. He only says: after all that, do not believe it is true. But belief has already played its functional role. The scolding comes too late.


6. THE ECONOMICS OF EPISTEMIC PRODUCTION

An economic analogy illuminates the structure of the problem. In productive economies, surplus is not a luxury—it is the condition of production itself. An economy that operates at bare subsistence cannot accumulate capital, cannot invest in future production, cannot sustain itself across disruptions. The demand for zero surplus is a demand for economic collapse.

Cognition exhibits the same structure. Knowledge is not a static inventory but a dynamic process of projection, correction, and accumulation. This process requires investment—commitment that exceeds current returns. We must believe more than we know in order to come to know anything. We must risk error in order to achieve truth.

The austerity advocate who demands that belief not exceed entitlement is like an economic planner who demands that consumption not exceed current production. The demand sounds prudent. But it prohibits precisely the investment that makes future production possible. It mistakes the avoidance of risk for the achievement of stability.

Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism is economic policy for epistemology—and it is the wrong policy. It demands that cognition operate at break-even, generating no surplus. But break-even cognition is bankrupt cognition. There is no such thing as a minimal viable epistemology.


7. EPISTEMIC RENT-SEEKING

We can now give a precise name to what constructive empiricism represents: epistemic rent-seeking.

In economic terms, rent-seeking is the extraction of value without contributing to production. The rent-seeker benefits from an economy while refusing to participate in the investments that make the economy possible.

Constructive empiricism exhibits exactly this structure at the epistemic level. It extracts normative authority from realist cognitive practice—the practice that generates stable concepts, reliable inference patterns, successful predictions, and a shared world of discourse—while disavowing the commitments that make that practice possible.

The constructive empiricist benefits from the results of surplus-generating cognition. She uses world-directed concepts (mouse, electron, tumor, quark). She relies on counterfactual reasoning. She presupposes object persistence. She trusts predictions. She operates within a framework that realist cognition has stabilized. But she denies the legitimacy of the investments that created that framework.

The position presents itself as intellectual humility. But it functions as free-riding on earned structure. It is, in the precise sense developed here, parasitic.

The analogy sometimes offered is apt: constructive empiricism is like being heir to a fortune and advocating communism. The heir can afford to disdain wealth because wealth already exists. Van Fraassen can afford to counsel belief-restraint because belief-surplus has already generated the cognitive economy within which restraint can be articulated.


8. THE ASYMMETRY OF RISK

There is a sociological dimension to the appeal of epistemic austerity that deserves mention. Within protected intellectual environments—stable institutions, shared disciplinary norms, no personal stakes tied to being right about the world—error is cheap and skepticism is costless.

In such environments, withholding belief has no downside. Practice goes on regardless of what one believes about the ultimate nature of unobservables. The scientist continues to make predictions, the engineer continues to build bridges, the physician continues to treat patients—and the philosopher can enjoy the luxury of doubt about whether any of these activities involve genuine knowledge of mind-independent reality.

But outside such environments—where cognition must actually work, where being wrong has consequences, where the difference between truth and error matters—the same posture is revealed as untenable. The agent who refuses to commit beyond strict entitlement cannot act, cannot learn, cannot intervene.

This explains the biographical puzzle raised in earlier discussions: why do extremely intelligent people end up endorsing positions like constructive empiricism? The answer is that the positions are affordable only from a position of epistemic privilege. The academic philosopher has already inherited the cognitive capital generated by surplus-producing cognition. From that secure vantage, restraint looks like rigor rather than refusal.

Van Fraassen never had to live with the consequences of his epistemology. Someone who did—someone whose survival depended on getting the structure of reality right—would find constructive empiricism not just implausible but unusable.


9. THE GROUND-FLOOR TEST

A crucial test of any epistemological position is whether it can be adopted at the beginning of inquiry rather than only after inquiry has succeeded. Epistemic austerity fails this test decisively.

Imagine a cognitive agent attempting to operate according to austerity principles from the start. Such an agent could not form the concept of an object (surplus over sense data), could not posit causal relations (surplus over observed regularities), could not project patterns to unobserved cases (surplus over finite evidence), could not trust memory (surplus over present experience), and could not engage in language (surplus over acoustic or visual stimuli).

The agent would not achieve a leaner, more disciplined form of cognition. The agent would achieve nothing at all. There would be no beliefs to restrain, no commitments to minimize, no epistemology to economize.

Constructive empiricism, like all austerity positions, presupposes a functioning epistemic economy before it can be voiced. It cannot be the policy that generates that economy. It can only be a post hoc tax on the wealth that realist cognition has already created.


10. FROM SPECIFIC FAILURE TO GENERAL IMPOSSIBILITY

The argument of this paper can now be summarized. Constructive empiricism fails for a specific reason: it instantiates the general impossibility of epistemic austerity. The austerity demand—that belief not exceed strict entitlement—is incoherent because:

Cognition requires surplus. Prediction, explanation, object-commitment, counterfactual reasoning, and even basic perception all involve commitments that exceed what evidence strictly warrants.

The concepts that articulate austerity are themselves surplus. Evidence, entitlement, adequacy, observation—these are not given in experience but imposed on it. They presuppose the modal, world-directed structure that austerity purports to restrain.

Austerity positions can only be stated after surplus-generating cognition has succeeded. They are retrospectively parasitic on the very mode of cognition they claim to discipline.

Therefore, austerity positions are forms of epistemic rent-seeking. They extract normative authority from realist cognitive practice while disavowing responsibility for the conditions that make that practice possible.

Constructive empiricism is the most sophisticated contemporary version of epistemic austerity. Its sophistication does not save it from the general failure. Van Fraassen's brilliance is real, but it is brilliance in the service of an impossible ideal.


11. CONCLUSION: INVESTMENT, NOT ABSTINENCE

The lesson is not that epistemic discipline is unimportant. It is that discipline must be understood as wise investment rather than austerity.

The question is not how to minimize commitment but how to commit productively—how to generate, allocate, and compound epistemic capital in ways that yield genuine understanding. Surplus is not noise to be eliminated but capital to be invested. The goal is not to approach zero but to invest wisely—to fund productive commitments and divest from sterile ones.

Constructive empiricism mistakes abstinence for achievement. It thinks that refusing to draw conclusions equals being rigorous. But philosophy is not bookkeeping. At some point, not explaining is a failure to explain, not a virtue.

A philosophy of science should illuminate why cognition works, why concepts latch onto the world, why prediction is possible at all. Constructive empiricism explicitly declines that job. It offers only the injunction to stop asking.

The position is internally coherent. Van Fraassen's arguments are careful, subtle, and consistent. But he chose skepticism-without-panic over explanation-with-risk. Once that choice is rejected, the entire structure collapses.

What remains is the real work of epistemology: not the avoidance of risk, but the intelligent bearing of it.
