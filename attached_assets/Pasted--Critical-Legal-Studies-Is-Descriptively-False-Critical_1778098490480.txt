# Critical Legal Studies Is Descriptively False

Critical Legal Studies (CLS) was a movement in legal academia that peaked in the 1970s and 1980s. Its central claim was that law is politics, doctrine is ideology, and the appearance of neutral legal reasoning is a mask for power. Duncan Kennedy at Harvard was the most visible figure. The movement positioned itself as the radical wing of the legal academy.

The movement was wrong. Not tonally wrong, not insufficiently nuanced, but descriptively wrong about the thing it claimed to describe. Get involved in an actual legal case—as a litigant, as someone falsely accused, as someone correctly accused, as a beneficiary, as a defendant—and you will find that the law is not what the judge ate for breakfast. There is a structure. The structure has two layers. CLS missed both.

## The Two Layers

The first layer is the autopilot. The vast majority of what the legal system does, it does mechanically. Filings get docketed. Motions get processed under standards the judge has applied a thousand times. Default judgments enter when answers don't. Summary judgment grants or denies based on whether there is a genuine issue of material fact. Routine motions to dismiss get ruled on with very little play in the joints. The judge is not consulting his ideology when he applies the standard for a motion to vacate a settlement. He is applying the standard, and the ruling falls where the doctrine puts it.

This layer is enormous. Most of what happens in courthouses every day happens this way. A competent lawyer can predict outcomes in routine matters with reasonable accuracy. If she could not, the entire industry of settlement negotiation, insurance underwriting of litigation risk, and contingency-fee evaluation would not function. That industry is huge and reasonably accurate. CLS had to ignore it to maintain its thesis.

The second layer is discretion. Judges have it. They exercise it. The bad outcomes cluster here, because the autopilot is mostly executing rules while discretion is where the judge's judgment enters and where things can go in directions the rules don't compel. CLS noticed this layer, mistook it for the whole system, and argued that the autopilot layer was also discretion in disguise. That is the indeterminacy thesis, and it is false.

## What CLS Got Wrong About Discretion

Even within the discretionary layer, CLS got the picture wrong. Discretion is not pell-mell. It is not unprincipled. Judges exercising discretion cluster around statistical norms. The judge handling a divorce case is not making it up from scratch; he is operating within a distribution of how judges in that jurisdiction tend to handle similar fact patterns, and his ruling will usually fall within that distribution. The same is true for fiduciary cases, contract disputes, criminal sentencing within the ranges the guidelines allow.

This means the typical pathology of legal injustice is not what CLS claimed. The typical pathology is not "the judge ruled to serve power." The typical pathology is regression to the mean in a case that required an outlier ruling. The case has features that should pull it out of the cluster—specific bad faith, specific misconduct, specific procedural pattern—and the judge instead applies the statistically normal response, because judges, like all bureaucratic actors, default to treating cases as typical unless something forces them to do otherwise.

Take a recent example from a trust administration matter. The trust language was mandatory: "shall distribute outright and free of trust." The trustee made zero distributions for over a year. Five of six relevant accounts had been concealed from the beneficiary's prior counsel. A seven-figure portfolio liquidation generated avoidable capital gains. Seven million dollars had been moved from the still-living settlor's brokerage account to an institution where the trustee's daughter worked, before the trustee was even formally trustee. A document submitted to the court purporting to show a freeze letter from the brokerage was missing the date. Twenty thousand dollars had been paid to an out-of-state lawyer who performed no identifiable estate administration work.

These are outlier features. A case with this set of facts is not the median trust dispute. It is a case where the median trustee-deferential disposition produces a wrong result.

What happened at the hearing was instructive. The judge had read the filings. He was not hostile to the beneficiary. He explicitly said, on the record, that he was not suggesting the objections lacked merit. But his procedural disposition was the median disposition for warring-sibling trust cases: get the accounting in, set objections, set a hearing, restrict volume of filings, move toward distribution. The outlier features—the concealment, the liquidation, the pre-trusteeship transfer, the dated document, the no-work payment—were not addressed at the hearing. They were deferred to the objections filing, where they will compete with everything else for the court's attention.

This is not the law serving power in the CLS sense. It is the law processing a case according to the statistical pattern when the case has features that should pull it out of the pattern. The judge is not bought, not mean, not corrupt, not stupid. He is doing what judges do, which is handle the case in front of him within the discretionary range that judges in his position handle such cases. That range was developed in a population of cases where most trustees are not behaving as this one is behaving. The system is calibrated for the median trustee. The bad-faith trustee benefits from that calibration.

CLS had nothing to say about this. Its framework could not generate the analysis. The framework was built to demonstrate that law is politics in the abstract, not to identify the specific mechanisms by which legal outcomes diverge from formal merits in individual cases. The actual mechanisms are statistical, procedural, and resource-driven, and they require empirical work to map. CLS did not do empirical work. CLS produced theoretical articles.

## The Resource Asymmetry

The other thing CLS missed, or got wrong by importing the wrong vocabulary, is the resource asymmetry that determines which cases can be pulled out of the statistical cluster.

Pulling a case out of the cluster requires sustained legal work. Specific facts have to be documented. Specific arguments have to be briefed. Specific evidentiary records have to be built. The judge has to be shown, repeatedly and over time, that this case is not the median case. That work takes lawyers, and the lawyers who can do it well are not free.

In any case where one side is a fiduciary represented by an established firm, and the other side is a beneficiary or other individual party, the asymmetry is severe. The firm has the resources to file slowly and steadily, to brief every issue thoroughly, to control the pace of the litigation. The individual party often has to choose between proceeding pro se, which means doing the work without legal training, or hiring an attorney whose competence is bounded by what the individual can afford.

There is a further asymmetry that CLS especially missed. Lawyers at established firms know who they will see across the table next year. They know who they will need a favor from. Taking a case against a peer firm in a way that actually fights, rather than performs fighting, has career costs that the individual lawyer correctly assesses. The result is that respectable lawyers will often not take cases against other respectable lawyers, or will take them and pull punches. The lawyers willing to fight hard are typically on the edges of the system—solos, small firms, lawyers who have already burned bridges with the establishment. Some are excellent. Many are erratic or undercapitalized. They are the only ones who will actually point a weapon at the people on the other side, because the people on the other side are not their colleagues and future referral sources.

This is the actual political economy of legal practice. It is not the bourgeois apparatus reproducing capitalist class relations. It is something more specific and more analyzable: a guild structure in which the credentialed members extend each other professional courtesies that have measurable effects on case outcomes, and in which clients who lack access to that guild structure absorb the costs.

CLS could not see this clearly because CLS was inside the guild. The CLS professoriate was at Harvard, Yale, Stanford, the Wisconsin and Buffalo programs at their peak. They were tenured. Their students went on to staff the firms whose practices they theoretically critiqued. The movement's funding, platform, and audience all came from inside the institutions it claimed to expose. It was always going to mis-locate the problem because locating the problem accurately would have required identifying the role of its own host institutions, and that identification was not on offer.

## The Marxist Tethering

The second-order failure of CLS, on top of getting the empirical picture wrong, was that it could not articulate even its partial insights in any vocabulary other than the Marxist one its authors had absorbed in graduate school. The observation that legal outcomes correlate with class power got translated into "the legal system is a bourgeois apparatus reproducing capitalist class relations." The translation lost most of the analytical purchase.

The first formulation invites empirical investigation. How exactly does the advantage operate? In which kinds of cases? Through which mechanisms? What reforms would reduce it? You can do work on those questions, generate findings, propose interventions, test them.

The second formulation forecloses investigation by providing a totalizing answer that explains everything and therefore nothing. Once you have decided that the legal system is the bourgeois apparatus, you have no further work to do. The conclusion is the analysis. Every outcome confirms the thesis. Every counterexample is a special case. The framework cannot be falsified, which means it cannot be useful.

This is why CLS produced so little that practitioners or reformers could use. A real analysis of how the legal system disadvantages individual parties against institutional ones would have generated specific findings: about the statistical patterns of fiduciary deference, about the asymmetric resources for discovery, about the way fee structures shape settlement pressure, about the specific procedural devices that institutional defendants exploit, about how appellate review fails to correct certain kinds of trial-level injustices. That analysis would be useful. It would tell a litigant which procedural battles are worth fighting, which arguments have empirical traction, which reforms would actually shift outcomes.

CLS produced almost none of this. It produced articles about the indeterminacy of doctrine, the fundamental contradiction, the ideological character of legal categories. The articles were citationally productive within the academy and analytically useless outside it.

## What the Realists Already Had

The legal realists, fifty years before CLS, had pointed at exactly the right targets. Holmes, Frank, Llewellyn, the rest of them: they identified the gap between formal doctrine and actual outcomes, the role of judicial psychology and discretion, the ways in which legal categories conceal political choices. The realists were going somewhere. Their project, if continued, would have led to empirical legal studies, to public-interest litigation strategy, to structural reform analysis.

CLS could have continued that project. It chose not to. It chose instead to import continental theory and attach the realist insights to a Marxist superstructure they didn't fit. The succession failed. The realists' grandchildren should have been people who could do empirical work on how courts actually function. Instead they were professors giving seminars on the fundamental contradiction.

## What's Actually Wrong With the Law

There is a great deal that is genuinely wrong with the American legal system. The substantive law has serious problems. The legal process has different and often worse problems. CLS did not cleanly identify a single one of them.

Some of what is actually wrong:

Procedural rules systematically favor parties with resources. Discovery is expensive, and the side that can afford more of it has an advantage independent of the merits. Motion practice can be deployed to exhaust an underfunded opponent. Continuances and scheduling controls advantage parties who can wait.

Fee structures shape settlement pressure asymmetrically. A contingency-fee plaintiff and a billing-hour defendant have different incentives at every stage. A pro se litigant and a represented party have different effective resources.

Judges are calibrated to median cases. Outlier cases, where the median disposition produces a wrong result, require the disfavored party to do disproportionate work to force the court to see the case as outlier rather than typical.

Professional protective networks reduce the supply of effective counsel for parties suing institutional defendants. The respectable bar will often not aggressively represent clients against other respectable lawyers. The aggressive lawyers are often outside the respectable bar for reasons that include both their willingness to fight and their lack of the resources and polish that come with respectability.

Appellate review does not correct most trial-level errors. The standards of review are deferential, the cost of appeal is high, and the rate at which trial-level outcomes are reversed is low. The trial court is, for most purposes, the final court.

Information asymmetries are vast. Institutional litigants have repeat-player knowledge that individual litigants lack. They know which judges grant which motions, which arguments work in which courtrooms, how to time filings, how to phrase requests for relief. The individual litigant, even with competent counsel, is at an information disadvantage that no amount of legal research can fully close.

These are real, specific, analyzable problems. They have causes. They have potential remedies. They are also not the problems CLS was looking at. CLS was looking at the problem of doctrinal indeterminacy and the problem of the law's claim to neutrality, both of which are at best minor side issues compared to the structural problems above.

## Conclusion

CLS gave its readers a vocabulary for sounding sophisticated about the law's general implication in power, while leaving them completely unequipped to identify the specific mechanisms by which power operates in specific cases. That is a poor return on the intellectual investment the movement asked of its readers.

A pro se litigant in a trust dispute, a small-business owner facing a contract suit, a tenant in eviction proceedings, a worker in a wage-and-hour case, a beneficiary trying to extract distributions from a non-distributing fiduciary—these are the people who actually need accurate analysis of how the legal system functions. CLS gave them nothing. The framework's vocabulary, applied to their cases, generates phrases but not insights. They learn to call their experience "structural" or "ideological" without learning anything about which procedural moves might help them, which arguments have traction, which lawyers will actually fight, which evidentiary patterns will pull their case out of the statistical cluster.

The work CLS did not do still needs doing. It will not be done by the heirs of Duncan Kennedy. It will be done, if at all, by people who treat the legal system as an empirical object whose actual operation can be mapped, whose actual mechanisms can be identified, whose actual asymmetries can be measured. That work will not sound radical. It will sound technical. It will produce findings that can be used. That is the test CLS could not pass and did not try to pass. It was a movement organized around the production of academic prose, not around the analysis of how courts actually work, and the prose has not aged well because it was never about what it claimed to be about.
