The Asymmetry of Prediction and Explanation
Prediction and explanation are commonly held to be structurally symmetrical. On the standard view, to predict is to use causal laws to map the present onto the future, and to explain is to use those same laws to map the present onto the past. The deductive skeleton is the same in both cases; only the temporal direction differs. This symmetry thesis, codified by Hempel and Oppenheim and defended in various forms for over half a century, is false. It is false not because the deductive skeleton is wrong, but because it is irrelevant. The actual inferential situations facing the predictor and the explainer are not symmetrical, and the asymmetry between them is not a marginal pragmatic wrinkle but a structural feature of the inferences themselves.
The asymmetry is this. To predict, one must know the relevant causal laws, the relevant present conditions, and that no unlisted condition will drown out the projection from present to future. Call this third requirement the closure premise. The closure premise is almost never satisfied, and when it is satisfied, it is almost never knowably so. Smith feeds his rabbit, knows rabbit biology, and infers that the rabbit will be alive tomorrow. The inference is valid given the factors Smith has identified. It is also unreliable, because the rabbit may be hit by a car—and Smith has no way to certify, in advance, that no car-equivalent lies outside his model. Every real system is open in this sense. Prediction therefore requires a modal guarantee about the absence of interferers, and such guarantees are categorically unavailable except in artificially sealed environments.
Explanation faces no such requirement. The present is, by definition, the residue of whatever did and did not get drowned out. The explainer does not have to prove that nothing intruded; the world has already run the experiment, and the outcome encodes the answer. To explain, one runs the laws backward from a known effect to a sufficient antecedent. If the backward map is many-to-one—if the same effect could have issued from several distinct mechanisms—the resulting ambiguity is not a closure problem but a resolution problem. Smith is dead: many mechanisms could have produced this. The coroner performs an autopsy: now there are far fewer. Resolution is solved by finer-grained observation, which is always in principle available and often in practice cheap. Closure cannot be solved at all.
The asymmetry, stated tightly, is therefore as follows. Prediction requires a closure premise (nothing outside the model will intrude) plus a weighting premise (the relative magnitudes of the operative factors are specified in advance). Explanation requires neither. It requires only identification of the mechanism that was in fact operative, and the explanandum itself supplies the evidence needed to fix the weights and, where necessary, to expand the inventory of operative factors. Jim, who is in mediocre shape, goes for a jog and dies of a coronary. Before the jog, we could not have predicted this outcome: we knew the operative factors but not their relative strengths, and we had no warrant to rule out interferers. After the event, we explain it without difficulty: the outcome itself tells us which factor dominated, and if a latent valve defect turns out to have been involved, the autopsy adds it to the inventory. The predictor must commit, in advance, to a complete and correctly weighted causal model of an open system. The explainer must only look at what is in front of him and look more closely when needed.
The consequence is that prediction and explanation are not two applications of the same inferential procedure run in opposite directions. They are inferences of categorically different kinds. Prediction is a modal undertaking: it demands knowledge of what is not the case (no interferers, no unlisted factors, no mis-weighted coefficients). Explanation is an epistemic undertaking: it demands only progressively finer knowledge of what is the case. The first is, in general, impossible. The second is, in general, tractable. The Hempelian symmetry thesis survives only in the limit where the causal inventory is complete, all weights are specified with arbitrary precision, and the system is known to be sealed. No interesting case satisfies these conditions, and the literature that has spent decades elaborating the symmetry thesis—together with its various reference-class refinements, screening-off conditions, and pragmatic side-constraints—has obscured rather than clarified the underlying point. The point is simple. Running the laws forward requires a guarantee one cannot have. Running the laws backward requires no such guarantee, because the present is what closure, whatever it amounted to, produced.
