The Catechism of the Pseudo-DefectorThe lawyers who present themselves as critics of the law repeat a small number of claims that are not critiques but advertisements. These claims have become so standard that they function as a catechism: the entry-level performance of mild dissent that establishes the speaker's bona fides as a thoughtful insider while leaving every structural feature of the profession untouched. Three are worth examining in detail.The first is the claim that lawyers are "competitive." The kid of the lawyer says it at the dinner table — "my dad is super competitive, that's why he became a lawyer." The lawyer-YouTuber says it as a self-description. The legal-aid memoirist says it about the colleagues she is supposedly criticizing — "the firm I left was hyper-competitive, that wasn't the kind of lawyer I wanted to be." The word does specific rhetorical work and the work is fraudulent.The insinuation behind "competitive" is that the lawyer fights for his client. That when retained, he becomes an instrument of the client's interest, willing to outwork and outthink the opposing side to deliver a result the client wants. The image is of the gun-for-hire, the hired blade, the professional who will go further for his client than the client could go for himself. This image is what the profession sells, and what the kid of the lawyer is repeating when he describes his dad as competitive.The image is false at every level. Lawyers do not score clean victories. They do not, in the overwhelming majority of cases, do anything that an external observer could identify as winning. What they do is delay, obfuscate, file motions, request continuances, negotiate settlements neither party can independently evaluate, and produce paper that closes out files in ways that allow both sides' lawyers to bill and both sides' clients to be told that something was achieved. The "competition" is between lawyers who share a common interest in keeping the apparatus running. The opposing lawyer is not the enemy. The opposing lawyer is the colleague at the next conference table, the person whose continued retention depends on the file remaining alive long enough to bill, the future referral source, the present co-conspirator in the management of the dispute.When the lawyer's child describes the lawyer as competitive, he is repeating something he was told. What he actually watched, if he was paying attention, was his father on conference calls, drafting documents, attending status conferences, responding to emails. Nothing about that work resembles competition in any domain where the word is doing real work. A chess player competes. A trader competes. A surgeon performing a procedure where the outcome is binary and visible competes. A lawyer attends. He shows up. He processes. The performance of vigor in court — the occasional cross-examination, the closing argument, the heated motion practice — is a small minority of the actual work, and when it does occur, it produces outcomes that no external observer can readily evaluate against any baseline.The second function of "competitive" is to flatter the lawyer's self-image and conceal the reality. The word lets the lawyer adopt the affect of an athlete or a trader without doing anything that requires his nervous system to be on the line. There is no scoreboard. The wins are settlements nobody can independently grade. The losses are losses. The mythology of the closing argument that broke the witness is statistically negligible against the reality of the office park and the deposition transcript. The kid of the lawyer believes the affect because the affect was performed for him at the dinner table.The euphemism's deepest function is to substitute the appearance of strenuous client advocacy for the reality of slow processing. Emrick, in the case I have been litigating, performs competitiveness in court. He gestures, he objects, he characterizes opposing arguments as frivolous, he flatters the judge. He is not fighting for his client. He is billing the file. His client, Carolina, would end up with more money — substantially more — if he disappeared tomorrow and a competent routine probate lawyer replaced him. The "competition" is not delivering value to her. It is delivering revenue to him under a description that allows her to believe she has a competent advocate. The performance of competitiveness is what makes the extraction palatable. Without the performance, the extraction would be visible. With the performance, it reads as the necessary cost of vigorous representation.The second standard lie is that lawyers are drawn from "the best in their class," the "smartest" graduates of the elite universities, that the profession is a meritocracy of the most intellectually capable. This claim is repeated by lawyers when describing themselves, by law-school admissions offices, by judges in commencement addresses, by the journalists who cover the legal profession. It is false at multiple levels.What the profession selects for is not intelligence in any defensible sense. It selects for the capacity to perform a specific kind of credential-accumulation: high enough grades in college, a passable LSAT, the ability to produce law-school exam answers that conform to the expectations of the professors grading them, and the social-affiliational skills to acquire summer associate positions, references, and ultimately job offers. None of these tests measure intelligence. They measure the capacity to absorb and reproduce institutional expectations.The genuinely intelligent person — the person whose mind is actually doing first-order work on problems rather than producing institutional outputs — runs into trouble in this environment. He notices that the cases he is reading don't actually support the doctrines they are taught as supporting. He notices that the standard moves on the exam don't work for the questions actually being asked. He produces answers in his own words rather than in the seminar's words, and the answers receive worse grades than less-thoughtful ones that conform to the expected vocabulary. He learns this fast or doesn't learn it. If he learns it, he stops thinking on purpose. If he doesn't learn it, he washes out or settles for lower-tier credentials.The lawyers who advance — who make partner, who become judges, who become law professors — are predominantly B-minus minds with A-level credential-grooming capacities. They are people who absorbed the institutional expectations early, performed them consistently, never disrupted anything, and accumulated the markers of success that the institution rewards. Their intelligence is moderate. Their conformity is exceptional. The combination is what produces the appearance of intellectual achievement without the substance of it.This is why senior figures in the legal profession are so often visibly poor at the basic operations they are supposed to have mastered. Watch a federal appellate judge struggle to articulate, in non-doctrinal language, what a case is actually about. Watch a name-partner at a major firm try to explain to a non-lawyer what the legal theory of his big case actually is. The performance is, in most cases, embarrassing. The senior figure can recite doctrine. He cannot, in many cases, do the thinking the doctrine is supposed to be a record of. He never had to. The career rewarded the recitation and the conformity, not the doing. By the time he is senior, the doing has atrophied if it was ever present.The lie about lawyer-intelligence is structurally important because it underwrites the prestige of the profession and the deference that prestige extracts. The judge is treated as wise because the judge is presumed to have been selected from among the most capable. The biglaw partner is paid eight hundred dollars an hour because his clients are told he is among the brightest in his field. The law professor is granted authority over what counts as legal knowledge because the law schools are presented as filtering for genius. Strip the lie and the prestige collapses. The judge is a B-minus mind with a robe. The partner is a B-minus mind with a corner office. The professor is a B-minus mind with a citation count. The profession cannot tolerate this recognition because the recognition would defund the prestige and, eventually, the salaries.The third standard lie is the explanation of why lawyering is bad that ex-lawyers and almost-ex-lawyers offer when asked. The explanation invariably involves "long hours," "high stress," "demanding clients," "work-life balance." The lawyer who left BigLaw at thirty-two writes about the toll of the eighty-hour weeks, the missed birthdays, the depression and the substance abuse. The legal-aid lawyer writes about burnout from the caseload and the inadequacy of the systems she was trying to navigate. The mid-career lawyer who switched to consulting writes about how the billable hour destroyed her relationships.Every one of these complaints applies, with equal or greater force, to many other professions. Surgeons work long hours. Investment bankers work longer. Tenure-track scientists in productive labs work continuously. Special-forces operators, journalists on hostile assignments, founders of startups, ER doctors, on-call obstetricians — the list of professions where the hours are punishing is long and the law is in the middle of the list, not at the top. If "long hours" were the actual problem, the literature would be a literature about professional overwork generally, and there would be nothing distinctive about lawyer-complaint in it.What the "long hours" complaint is actually doing is providing a safe explanation for leaving the profession that does not require the speaker to confront what the profession is. The lawyer who quits at thirty-two and says "the hours were killing me" does not have to say "I realized the work was the production of paper that destroyed people's lives in service of clients I despised." The first explanation is socially acceptable, professionally portable, and consistent with continuing to maintain bar membership and pleasant relations with former colleagues. The second explanation would close every door the lawyer might want to walk through later. The first explanation is therefore what gets written, and the second explanation does not appear in the published literature at all.This is the deepest function of the "long hours" complaint: it lets the departing lawyer leave without naming what they saw. The departure is recoded as a personal-lifestyle decision rather than a moral recognition. The profession is preserved as a noble undertaking that simply demands too much of its practitioners. Other practitioners are flattered by this framing — they are the strong ones who can take the hours. The leaver is positioned as the sensitive one who made a different choice. Nobody has to confront the question of what the hours were producing or whom they were producing it against.These three lies — competitiveness, intelligence, long hours — are the catechism. They are repeated by everyone in and adjacent to the profession because they are the formulas that allow continued participation in the institutional apparatus without recognition of what the apparatus does. The lawyer who repeats them is signaling that he is a member in good standing. The lawyer who refuses to repeat them is signaling something the profession cannot absorb, and is therefore not heard. The result is a literature of millions of words in which these three claims appear continuously and in which the underlying realities they conceal are never named. The catechism is what fills the empty shelf described in Essay 1.
