The Cop, the Lawyer, and the Difference Between Surface Corruption and Total CorruptionA cop pulls a driver over for speeding. The driver offers him five thousand dollars not to write the ticket. The cop takes it. The driver drives away. The state's traffic enforcement system is mildly degraded; the driver has obtained a small illegal benefit; the cop has obtained an illegal cash payment. This is corruption in the standard sense, and it is mickey mouse.It is mickey mouse because the cop's actual function — enforcing traffic law, investigating crimes, keeping order — exists independently of the bribe. The bribe is a deviation from the function. Subtract the bribe and the cop is still a cop, still does the job, still produces the legitimate output of his role for the vast majority of his interactions. The corruption is a tax on the legitimate function. The function survives the corruption with most of its integrity intact. You can describe what the cop is supposed to do without referring to the bribe. You can audit his work by counting the cases where the bribe was not paid and the function was performed as designed. The vocabulary of corruption-versus-function works cleanly here.A lawyer is hired by a client in a settlement negotiation. The lawyer is paid a fee by the client. The lawyer negotiates a settlement that is significantly worse for the client than what was available at the time of retention, and significantly better for the opposing party than the opposing party expected to obtain. The lawyer is CC'd, throughout the negotiation, on a chain with opposing counsel. The settlement is presented to the client as the best available outcome. The client signs. The file closes. The lawyer collects his fee and moves on. The opposing party's counsel records a successful resolution. The opposing party retains assets they should have surrendered. Years later, the client realizes what happened and tries to undo the settlement, at significant cost and with limited prospects of success.This is the Colby pattern, and it is not mickey mouse. It is the deep form of corruption that the cop's bribe is a degraded shadow of.The crucial difference: subtract the corruption from the cop and a real job remains. Subtract the corruption from Colby and nothing remains. The "function" Colby performed, in the official description, was to advocate for his client. What he actually performed was the delivery of his client into a settlement that benefited the opposing party. These are not the same function with a corruption-layer on top. They are opposite functions. The "corruption" did not deviate from the legitimate work; it replaced the legitimate work entirely. There is no Colby-doing-the-job-correctly to compare against Colby-doing-the-job-corruptly, because the job, as performed, was the corruption. The fee Colby collected from his client was payment for the betrayal of the client. The client paid for his own defeat. The transaction was complete and self-contained.This is why the bribe-vocabulary fails to describe what lawyers do. A bribe presupposes an underlying legitimate function being deflected by a side payment. The deflection is the corruption. The function is the baseline. With Colby, there is no baseline. The function is the deflection. There was never an underlying legitimate function that the corruption interrupted. The whole transaction, from initial retention through final settlement, was an unbroken arc of work that looked procedurally regular at every step and was substantively the inverse of what it was advertised to be.The cop who takes the five thousand dollars knows he is doing something wrong. Other cops would condemn him if it came out. The criminal code identifies his act as bribery. The internal affairs apparatus has procedures for investigating him. The disciplinary norms of his profession can locate the misconduct unambiguously. He has a moral position to occupy when he reflects on what he did, even if he chooses not to occupy it. The mirror is available; he can decline to look but he knows the mirror is there.Colby has no such mirror. His conduct, described to other lawyers, would not register as misconduct. He would describe the settlement at a bar dinner — "yeah, I had a difficult client who wanted to fight everything, but we got it closed at a number we could all live with" — and other lawyers would nod with sympathy and understanding. The conduct does not register as deviance within the professional community. It registers as competent practice. Closing the file. Managing a difficult client. Reaching an accommodation with opposing counsel. Every element of what Colby did is something the profession has explicit vocabulary to praise.The disciplinary apparatus, when it engages with Colby-conduct at all, can only sanction surface violations. Failure to communicate material terms. Failure to obtain informed consent. Inadequate documentation of strategic choices. None of these procedural deficiencies, even if proved, would touch the substance of what he did. The substance is unsanctionable because the substance is what most lawyers do most of the time in similar postures, and any rule that named it would dissolve the profession.This is the deepest sense in which legal practice is, structurally, what cop-bribery is only incidentally. The cop's profession exists; corruption is an external deformation of it. The lawyer's profession at its higher levels — corporate transactional work, settlement negotiation, trust and estate administration, fiduciary representation — does not exist apart from the structural corruption that constitutes it. The fee paid by the client is the bribe; the bribe is paid for the betrayal; the betrayal is sold as service. There is no after-the-bribe-is-removed version where you can see what the legitimate work would have looked like, because the legitimate work was never what was being performed.Consider the structural reasons this is possible. The client of a lawyer cannot evaluate the work product in real time. The client does not know what settlements were available. The client does not know what the opposing party's bottom line was. The client does not know what discovery would have revealed if it had been aggressively pursued. The client does not know what a competent advocate would have done differently. The client knows only what the lawyer tells him. The information asymmetry is total. It is not a flaw in the system; it is the system. The lawyer's institutional position is constituted by the asymmetry. The asymmetry is what allows him to charge fees that the client cannot calibrate against any meaningful baseline. Without the asymmetry, the profession in its current form would not exist.The cop and the driver, by contrast, can both see what happened. The driver knows he was speeding. The cop knows he wrote a ticket or didn't. The bribe was paid; both parties remember the amount. The transaction was symmetrical at the informational level. If the driver had been a passenger and not the bribe-payer, he could have witnessed the exchange. The cop's corruption is performable in the open in a way that Colby's corruption is structurally not.This is why prosecution of legal-profession misconduct is so vanishingly rare. The bar disciplinary apparatus catches the procedural-violation cases — the trust-account thefts, the missed statutes of limitations, the cases where the lawyer literally absconded with money. It does not and cannot catch the structural-betrayal cases, because the structural betrayal is what the profession produces every day. To sanction it would be to sanction the profession itself. The disciplinary apparatus is staffed by the profession, regulates the surface behaviors, and leaves the substantive transaction untouched. Every active member of the disciplinary apparatus is, in his own practice, doing some version of what Colby did to his client. They cannot see Colby's conduct as misconduct because they cannot afford to.The cop who takes the five thousand dollars is a discrete bad actor in a system that, on the whole, performs a real function. The function is mostly preserved; the corruption is the exception. The system can be reformed by removing the exception. The cop can be fired; the next cop in the seat will, statistically, behave better.Colby is not a discrete bad actor. He is a representative practitioner of his profession. The misconduct, in his case, is the profession's normal product. Removing him does nothing. The next lawyer in the seat will do the same thing, because the same incentive structure will be in place, the same fee will be paid by the same kind of client, and the same opposing counsel will be CC'd on the same emails, and the same settlement will get reached at the same suboptimal number, and the same client will sign and walk away. The reform that would address Colby-conduct would have to be a reform of the entire transactional structure of legal services, which is to say, it would have to be the dismantling of the profession in its current form. No internal reform can touch this. The disciplinary apparatus is constitutionally unable to see the misconduct, because the disciplinary apparatus is the profession looking at itself, and the profession's self-image is the apparatus that produced Colby and protects him.The bribe-taking cop is corrupt in a way the legal vocabulary can name, prosecute, and replace. Colby is corrupt in a way the legal vocabulary cannot name, prosecute, or replace, because Colby is the legal vocabulary in operation. The cop, in moral terms, is a small failure of a large system. Colby, in moral terms, is the large system, operating as designed, in one of its representative instances.A thousand times worse is roughly correct. The cop's wrong is contained. Colby's wrong is the field. The cop's wrong has an outside that can judge it. Colby's wrong has no outside, because the profession that defines what counts as wrong is the profession that produced him. He can go to his grave believing he did his job, and the bar will agree with him at his funeral.
