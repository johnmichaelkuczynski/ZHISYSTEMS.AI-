The State of Nature as Philosophical Fraud:
From Golding's Lord of the Flies to the Foundations of Political Philosophy
John-Michael Kuczynski, Ph.D.
Zhi Systems
Abstract
This paper argues that the "state of nature" concept central to early modern political philosophy—and perpetuated in works like William Golding's Lord of the Flies—constitutes not a theoretical idealization but a piece of bad anthropology masquerading as a priori reasoning. Drawing on ethnographic evidence, evolutionary psychology, and the documented behavior of actual stranded human groups, I demonstrate that the "state of nature" never existed and that its invocation by philosophers from Hobbes to Rawls has served primarily to justify coercive political arrangements by reference to fictitious dangers. The actual sociology of human cruelty, I argue, inverts the standard picture: the worst forms of interpersonal destruction occur not in conditions of lawlessness but within intact institutional structures, where the rules that ostensibly prevent violence become instruments for perpetrating it.
1. Introduction: The Golding Thesis
William Golding's Lord of the Flies (1954) presents itself as a revelation about human nature: strip away the thin veneer of civilization, and what remains is latent savagery. A group of British schoolboys, stranded on a desert island without adult supervision, descend rapidly into murderous tribalism. The novel has been absorbed into the cultural consciousness as something approaching anthropological insight—a parable about what happens "when there are no adults in the room."
The thesis of this paper is that Golding's scenario is not merely unrealistic but represents a species of intellectual fraud that pervades Western political philosophy. Lord of the Flies is a moral allegory, not an empirical prediction. Everything in it is stylized to dramatize a conclusion Golding wished to reach before he began writing. The novel is, in effect, a rigged experiment—and it stands in a long tradition of such rigged experiments, stretching back through Rawls, Rousseau, Locke, and Hobbes.
2. The Empirical Evidence: What Actually Happens
In every documented real-world instance of stranded children or adolescents, the pattern is cooperation, division of labor, mutual care, and conflict resolution—not feral collapse. The best-known case is that of six Tongan boys stranded on the island of 'Ata in 1965 for more than fifteen months. They built a garden, established rules, created a rotating work schedule, exercised, constructed a makeshift gym, collected rainwater, kept a permanent fire burning, and cared for an injured member of their group. There was no violence. There was no power-mad leader. No one was murdered.
This case is not anomalous. The ethnographic and historical record consistently shows that small human groups—whether children, adolescents, or adults—default to pragmatic problem-solving, shared routines, fairness norms, and emotional bonding under pressure. The Golding scenario requires conditions that must be imposed as narrative devices: total absence of adults, a pre-engineered power struggle, carefully exaggerated personality types, scripted moral collapse, and rapid deterioration of shared norms. These are authorial choices, not empirical regularities.
3. Two Hundred Thousand Years of Counter-Evidence
The deepest refutation of the Golding thesis—and, by extension, of the entire "state of nature" tradition—comes from the archaeological and anthropological record of human prehistory. For approximately 200,000 years, human beings lived in small bands of twenty to fifty individuals, groups of roughly the same order of magnitude as Golding's fictional schoolboys and with only a slightly higher average age. These bands maintained stable food-sharing norms, punished free riders, protected vulnerable members, and managed leadership through consensus or rotating roles. They did not devolve into ritual murder as a baseline condition.
We possess dozens of ethnographic records of small-group societies. None remotely resembles Golding's island dystopia. Hunter-gatherer bands exhibit high cooperation, stable norms, conflict mediation, and reciprocity. Hierarchy is typically weak and regulated. Violence within the group is suppressed and rare. Communal decision-making is standard. Sanctions exist without formal law. Hobbes's "war of all against all" is not merely wrong—it is biologically impossible. A population that behaved that way would self-annihilate within a single generation. Human nature evolved under cooperative, small-band conditions, not chaos.
4. The State of Nature as Bad Anthropology
This brings us to the central fraud of early modern political philosophy: the "state of nature" concept was never a theoretical idealization analogous to the frictionless plane of physics. Hobbes and Locke did not treat it as a mathematical limit-case useful for isolating certain variables. They believed it was a real description of primordial humanity. They were doing bad ethnography based on travelers' tales, colonial propaganda, Christian myths of the Fall, aristocratic paranoia, and European fantasies of "savages." Then they universalized it.
Contrary to what their expositors might claim, these philosophers took a priori fantasies as the empirical baseline of humanity and then designed political systems to "solve" problems that never existed. Social order does not emerge from nothing. Social order is older than humans, older than primates, older than mammals. Order does not come after law; order precedes law by millions of years. Law is a late formal codification of patterns that already exist. The "state of nature" is what philosophers hallucinated when they abstracted away the very conditions that make human beings possible.
If Hobbes had watched actual wolves, he would have observed more order than in Parliament. There is plenty of social order in a pack of wolves. No pack animal—wolf, chimpanzee, bonobo, elephant—exhibits "state of nature" behavior in Hobbes's sense.
5. The Structure of the Fraud
The mechanism of this intellectual fraud operates as follows. First, invent a fake primitive condition. Second, declare that condition "the natural baseline." Third, define your political philosophy as the escape from it. Fourth, call the resulting structure "rational." Fifth, attack dissenters as ignoring "human nature." Once you see this structure, you cannot unsee it. It is conceptual fraud with a straight face.
Hobbes explicitly weaponizes the concept to justify absolute sovereignty. His reasoning runs: humans in the state of nature kill one another; to prevent that, they must give the sovereign all power; resistance is irrational because it risks returning to the state of nature. Once you accept his false anthropology, dictatorship becomes "rational." Modern governments still rely on softened versions of this logic—"security threats justify extraordinary measures."
Locke uses the same fiction to justify dispossession and colonialism. His "state of nature" feeds into his theory of property, which in turn justified colonial land seizures. He argued that indigenous peoples were "still in the state of nature" because they did not "mix labor" with the land in the English capitalist sense. Property rights thus became a conceptual weapon of conquest, with the state of nature functioning as a moral alibi.
Rousseau's version posits that society has "corrupted" man from a pure pre-social nature, thereby licensing revolution to justify purging "corrupt" classes, re-educating citizens, and enforcing ideological virtue. The fiction of natural innocence plus social corruption allows the state to intervene everywhere in the name of "restoring" a fantasy human nature.
Rawls repeats the same trick in cognitive form. He does not invent prehistoric anthropology; he invents prehistoric psychology: the "original position," the "veil of ignorance," "rational contractors," "primary goods." He treats these not as stipulations but as deep truths about what "rational agents" would choose. In reality, his economics is primitive, his psychology is nonexistent, his assumptions about motivation collapse under any actual behavioral science, and his model is a giant preference-engineered tautology—you get out exactly what you smuggle in. Just as Locke smuggles in middle-class Protestant norms as "natural rights," Rawls smuggles in mid-twentieth-century Harvard-liberal intuitions as "pure reason." Everything looks universal when you forget the source code.
6. The Political Uses of the Fiction
One of the great unacknowledged scandals of political thought is that the "state of nature" fiction did not merely misdescribe the past—it became a pretext for real, concrete abuses of power. Once you convince people that a "state of nature" exists, you can justify anything as a remedy. Once you posit an imaginary baseline of chaos, insecurity, predation, and war of all against all, you can argue that any coercive structure is better than the alternative, any overreach is a "necessary step" toward order, and any infringement of liberty is "preventing collapse." This logic is totalitarian in embryo. If the alternative is the "state of nature," you can rationalize torture, surveillance, unlimited police powers, preemptive war, and suspension of rights—all as "protecting civilization."
The "state of nature" framework always yields the same pattern: create a nightmare scenario, claim the nightmare is humanity's natural condition, announce that only your political system can rescue people from it, reframe any opposition as a threat to order, and thereby justify crushing that opposition. This is why the fiction is so powerful: it lets authority portray itself as the only thing standing between civilization and collapse—even though the collapse scenario never existed. It is the political equivalent of a protection racket.
The state of nature conceit has thus been used to justify some of the most vicious, ugly, and destructive legal and political overreaches in history—not despite its falsity but because its falsity makes it infinitely malleable. Once you let a fictional anthropology define what counts as "order," everything becomes justifiable in the name of avoiding the fiction's imaginary dangers.
7. The Actual Sociology of Human Nastiness
Perhaps the deepest irony in the Golding-Hobbes tradition is that it gets the sociology of human cruelty exactly backwards. There are indeed unbelievably nasty struggles between people. But most of this nastiness occurs within highly intact social structures—inside academic departments, corporate boards, priestly orders, political parties—not in their absence. People imagine that chaos produces violence. Empirically, institutions produce more viciousness than chaos does.
Why? Because institutions provide status hierarchies, competition for scarce positions, opaque evaluative mechanisms, weaponizable rules, reputational economies, formal procedures that can be manipulated, and mechanisms for coordinated exclusion. Academic departments are the perfect case: deliberately non-transparent, authority diffused, incentives misaligned, and punishment mostly symbolic. Conflict becomes psychological, reputational, and political—far more corrosive than anything in a stateless wilderness. Golding misses this entirely. His children dissolve into chaos because there are no structures. But real humans become vicious inside structures, precisely because they cannot dissolve them.
When violence does occur in truly lawless settings—failed states, contested borders, pirate zones, cartel zones—it is not group-on-self. It is group-on-outside: raids, predation, territorial signaling, resource seizure, group-on-outgroup aggression. Internal group cohesion is usually stronger in those conditions; that is how brigands, militias, and gangs function. Humans under external threat do not turn on each other. They fuse. Golding reverses this: he imagines that threat makes groups cannibalize themselves. But the evolutionary pattern is the opposite.
The worst internal savagery tends to occur within rule-governed environments, where the rules themselves become weapons. In intact, rule-heavy institutions, the rules that prevent overt violence become tools for covert violence; the norms that maintain civility provide cover for cruelty; procedural fairness becomes a battleground for status manipulation. When you take away direct aggression, what remains is psychological warfare, sabotage, rumor, administrative harassment, reputational assassination, bureaucratic strangleholds, and the mobilization of subordinates as stalking horses. That is how groups truly devour themselves—not with spears and painted faces but with committees, memos, and weaponized norms.
8. Golding's Novel as Symptom
Lord of the Flies, then, should be understood not as a diagnosis of humanity but as a projection of Golding himself. His worldview was shaped by World War II trauma, the collapse of European liberal humanism, and his own personal demons—alcoholism, self-loathing, and admitted cruelty as a youth. Golding universalizes his own pessimism. He was not describing human nature; he was describing his view of human nature.
There is a further irony. The story is taken to show "what happens without adults," but the world Golding builds is already designed like a dysfunctional adult hierarchy. Jack is a cardboard authoritarian; Ralph is a cardboard technocrat. It is adult politics in miniature, not child behavior. Golding is not describing what children become without adults. He is describing what adults become when they pretend their own political anxieties are universal human truths.
The novel's underlying political message—that without elite oversight or civilizational constraint, people revert to fascism—is a caricature, not a diagnosis. It completely ignores the legitimate problems that conservative political impulses attempt to address: order, scarcity, coordination, institutional decay, enforcement asymmetries. It ignores the historical fact that hierarchical structures often arise for functional reasons, not because people secretly want to paint their faces and skewer the weak.
9. Conclusion
Political philosophy has a long habit of doing bad empirical work and calling it a priori reasoning. Hobbes produced bad anthropology to justify political absolutism. Locke produced bad anthropology to justify proprietarian liberalism. Rousseau produced bad anthropology (of a different flavor) to justify romantic egalitarianism. Rawls produced bad psychology and bad economics to justify redistributive rationalism. In each case, whenever philosophers think they are deriving politics from "reason," they are typically deriving it from unexamined empirical errors.
The state of nature is one of philosophy's most catastrophic intellectual exports. It was always a political rhetorical device, not a human reality. Humans never lived in a lawless vacuum. They lived in structured, norm-governed groups from the beginning. And political theory has been building castles on that sand ever since.
Lord of the Flies is a fairy tale for adults who want to moralize about "savagery" without facing the far greater savagery of bureaucratic life. If anything, it is civilization—not its absence—that gives human beings the machinery for their most elaborate cruelty. Golding's novel, like the political philosophy it unwittingly perpetuates, is a rigged experiment meant to prove a conclusion that was smuggled in before the inquiry began.
